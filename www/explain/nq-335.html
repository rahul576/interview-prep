As a technical lead, how do you divide responsibilities and make technical decisions?

Agile & Scrum · scenario · levels senior, lead

In one line

I divide responsibilities by strengths, growth goals and clear ownership areas, deliberately spreading knowledge to avoid single points of failure, and make technical decisions by framing the problem, comparing options and trade-offs with the team, using spikes or POCs when uncertain, documenting them as ADRs, and practising disagree and commit.

Detailed explanation

A technical lead's leverage comes from making the team effective, not from writing the most code. For responsibilities, I map upcoming work against people: who has the expertise, who wants to grow in that area, and who is overloaded. Each component or feature gets a clear owner end to end, design, implementation, tests and operability, because shared ownership often means nobody owns it. At the same time I avoid knowledge silos, a low bus factor, through pairing, rotation, code review and documentation. I keep myself on unblocking, reviews, cross-team alignment and the riskiest technical parts, and avoid hoarding the interesting work.

For technical decisions, I start by framing the problem: the goal, constraints such as latency, cost, compliance and deadlines, and what a good outcome looks like. Then the team generates options, and we compare trade-offs explicitly: complexity, cost, risk, operability, team skills, reversibility and fit with the wider architecture. When the uncertainty is high, a short spike or proof of concept with measurable criteria beats a long debate.

I distinguish reversible "two-way door" decisions, which should be made quickly and close to the work, from irreversible ones, which deserve more analysis and alignment with architects and stakeholders. I aim for consensus, but if we cannot reach it in reasonable time, I make the call, explain the reasoning, and we disagree and commit. Significant decisions are recorded as Architecture Decision Records (context, decision, consequences), so newcomers understand why, and we revisit a decision when the facts change.

How it works

  1. Map work against skills, growth goals and load; assign clear owners.
  2. Pair and rotate to spread knowledge; review bus factor regularly.
  3. For a decision, frame the problem, constraints and success criteria.
  4. Gather options with the team; compare trade-offs; spike when uncertain.
  5. Seek consensus; otherwise decide, explain, and disagree and commit.
  6. Record an ADR, align with stakeholders, and revisit when facts change.

Example

# ADR-012: Use PostgreSQL outbox + Debezium for order events

Status: Accepted (2026-03-04)
Context: Order service must publish events reliably; dual writes to DB
and Kafka caused lost events twice last quarter.
Options: (1) dual write with retries (2) transactional outbox + Debezium
(3) Kafka transactions only
Decision: Option 2. Atomic with the business write; team knows Postgres;
Debezium already runs for analytics.
Consequences: extra outbox table and connector to operate; events are
at-least-once, consumers must be idempotent.

Common mistakes

  • The lead takes all the hard or interesting work and becomes a bottleneck.
  • Decisions made alone and announced, which loses buy-in and insight.
  • Endless debates on reversible decisions.
  • No written record, so the same debate repeats every few months.
  • Re-opening committed decisions without new information.

How to answer in the interview

"For responsibilities, I look at people's strengths and growth goals and give clear end-to-end ownership of components or features. I deliberately pair and rotate so knowledge isn't concentrated in one person, and I keep myself on unblocking, reviews and the riskiest parts rather than taking all the interesting work. For decisions, I frame the problem and constraints, gather options with the team and compare trade-offs like cost, risk, operability and skills. If we are unsure, we run a short spike with clear success criteria. I aim for consensus; if we can't get there, I make the call, explain why, and we disagree and commit. Significant decisions go into ADRs and are aligned with architects, and we revisit them if the facts change."

Likely follow-up questions

  • A technical decision that turned out wrong? Explain the context, the signal that it was wrong, how you reversed or mitigated it, and what you changed in your process.
  • What is a two-way door decision? A reversible decision that can be made fast and changed later at low cost.
  • How do you handle a senior engineer who disagrees strongly? Listen, test the concern with data or a spike, decide transparently, and record the dissent in the ADR.
