A strong senior engineer on your team is consistently missing deadlines and seems disengaged. What do you do?

People Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Talk privately and early, ask before assuming, give specific feedback (SBI), find the root cause, agree a clear plan with support, follow up, and only then use the formal performance process if nothing improves.

Detailed explanation

A strong senior engineer who suddenly misses deadlines and seems disengaged is usually sending a signal. The behaviour changed, so something changed: personal circumstances, health or burnout, unclear goals, boredom with repetitive work, frustration about a decision or promotion, conflict in the team, or interest in leaving. The manager's first job is to find out which, not to jump straight to performance management.

Use a private one-on-one soon, before the pattern becomes entrenched or affects the team. Open with care and with facts, using Situation-Behaviour-Impact (SBI): "In the last two sprints, the API migration and the billing fix both slipped by a week; that delayed the mobile release." Then ask open questions and really listen. Avoid labels like "you seem lazy"; describe behaviour, not character.

The response depends on the cause. Personal matters may need temporary flexibility, leave or support from HR or an employee assistance programme. Burnout needs a reduced load and a look at on-call and overtime. Boredom calls for more challenging or different work, such as leading a design. Unclear priorities need clearer goals from you. Whatever the cause, agree specific expectations and the support you will provide, write them down, and schedule follow-ups. If performance still does not improve after a fair period, involve HR and use the formal performance improvement process, documented and consistent.

How it works

  1. Notice the pattern and collect concrete examples.
  2. Hold a private one-on-one; share observations with SBI.
  3. Ask open questions and listen for the root cause.
  4. Agree a plan: expectations, support, adjusted workload, timeline.
  5. Follow up weekly; recognise improvement.
  6. If no improvement, involve HR and start a fair, documented process.

Example

Short scenario: "Priya, a senior engineer, missed two deadlines in a month and went quiet in stand-ups. In our one-on-one I shared the two examples and their impact, then asked how things were going. She said she had been maintaining the same legacy service for 18 months and felt stuck. We agreed she would lead the design of the new event pipeline, hand over half the legacy work to a mid-level engineer she would mentor, and meet weekly. Within six weeks her delivery was back on track and she presented the design to the architecture forum."

Common mistakes

  • Waiting too long, hoping it resolves itself.
  • Giving vague feedback without examples.
  • Assuming the cause, or diagnosing personal issues yourself.
  • Discussing it in public or in group meetings.
  • Skipping documentation, making a later formal process unfair.

How to answer in the interview

"I would act early with a private one-on-one, and I would ask before assuming. I would share specific observations using situation, behaviour and impact, such as the last two deadlines and what they delayed, and then mostly listen. A sudden change in a strong performer usually has a cause: personal issues, burnout, unclear goals or boredom. Depending on what I learn, I would offer flexibility, rebalance their work, give them something more challenging, or clarify priorities. We would agree clear expectations and the support I will give, and I would follow up regularly. If nothing improves after a fair period, I would involve HR and use the formal process, documented and fair."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you give critical feedback to someone more experienced than you? Stay factual and curious; respect their expertise but be clear about expectations.
  • What if they say they are planning to leave? Understand why, fix what you can, and plan knowledge transfer.
  • How do you protect the rest of the team meanwhile? Rebalance critical work and keep commitments realistic without sharing private details.
