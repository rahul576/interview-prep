Tell me about a mistake you made at work and what you learned from it.

Learning from Failure · behavioral · levels entry, mid, senior, lead

In one line

Choose a real, meaningful mistake, own it without excuses, explain how you fixed it and limited the impact, what you learned, and the concrete change you made to prevent recurrence, using STAR.

Detailed explanation

Everyone makes mistakes; the interviewer is assessing self-awareness, honesty, accountability and growth. Red flags are claiming you have never made a real mistake, picking a disguised strength ("I work too hard"), blaming others, or telling a story where the damage was severe and you learned nothing.

Choose well: a genuine professional mistake with real but recoverable impact, ideally a few years old so you can show how you have changed. It should not involve integrity issues. Technical mistakes (a bad migration, a missed edge case, a wrong estimate) or process mistakes (not escalating early, assuming instead of asking) both work.

Own it: use "I", not "we" or "they". Briefly state what you did wrong and why it happened (for example, time pressure led you to skip testing on production-sized data), without excessive self-criticism.

Fix and contain: show how quickly you raised it (telling your lead immediately is a strength), and how you mitigated the impact.

Learn and prevent: the most important part. Go beyond "I'll be more careful": describe a systemic change such as a checklist, automated test, review step or pipeline guard, ideally one that helped the whole team. Mention evidence that it worked.

Keep the story balanced: roughly 20% situation, 30% fixing, 50% learning and prevention.

How it works

  1. Situation: context and the mistake in one or two sentences.
  2. Task: your responsibility.
  3. Action: owning it, escalating, mitigating.
  4. Result: impact contained.
  5. Learning and prevention: systemic change and how it helped since.

Example

Situation: Early in my career I wrote a database migration adding a column with a default value and an index to a 40-million-row orders table, tested only on a small staging database. Task: I was responsible for the release that evening. Action: When it ran in production, it locked the table for several minutes and checkout requests started timing out. I immediately told my lead in the incident channel, explained that my migration was the cause, and we rolled it back. I then rewrote it: adding the column without a default, backfilling in batches of 10,000 rows, and creating the index concurrently. It ran cleanly the next night. Result: Impact was limited to about six minutes of slow checkout during low traffic. Learning: I learned that database changes must be tested at production-like volume. I added a migration checklist to our pull request template (locking behaviour, batching, concurrent indexes) and an automated pipeline step that runs migrations against a masked, production-sized snapshot. In the following year that step caught two other risky migrations before release.

Common mistakes

  • "I can't think of a mistake" or a fake one.
  • Blaming teammates, tools or managers.
  • Choosing a catastrophic or unethical incident.
  • Spending all the time on the drama and none on learning.
  • Lessons like "be more careful" without concrete change.

How to answer in the interview

"Early in my career I ran a database migration in production without testing it on realistic data volume, and it locked a large table for several minutes, slowing checkout. I took responsibility immediately, told my lead, and we rolled back; I rewrote it to backfill in batches with a concurrent index, which ran cleanly the next night. The impact was a short slowdown. What I learned is that changes must be tested under production-like conditions, so I added a migration checklist and an automated pipeline test against a production-sized copy, which later caught two other risky migrations."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How did your team react? Supportive because I raised it fast; it became a team learning session.
  • What did you change in your process? The checklist and production-sized migration test, now standard.
  • A more recent mistake? Have a second, smaller example ready, perhaps about communication or estimation.
