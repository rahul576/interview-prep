Describe a time you disagreed with a teammate or your manager on a technical decision. How did you handle it?

Teamwork · behavioral · levels mid, senior, lead

In one line

Show you handled a technical disagreement respectfully and objectively: you listened to understand the other view, used data, a POC or a design doc to decide, reached a good outcome, and kept or improved the relationship.

Detailed explanation

Disagreements are normal in engineering teams; interviewers want to see how you handle them. They are looking for emotional maturity (no ego, no grudges), collaboration (seeking to understand), objectivity (data over opinions), and commitment once a decision is made, even if it goes against you.

Choose a real technical disagreement with meaningful stakes: a technology choice, an architecture decision, an approach to testing or a deadline-versus-quality trade-off. Avoid stories where the other person is portrayed as foolish, or where you "won" by escalating.

The key moves to describe are: listening first and restating their concern; finding shared goals; agreeing criteria for deciding; gathering evidence, such as a benchmark, proof of concept or design document; and reaching a decision, maybe a compromise or a choice by the decision owner. If the decision went against you, say how you disagreed and committed and supported it fully.

Close with the result for the project and the relationship, and a lesson learned, such as "framing disagreements around shared goals and data works better than arguing opinions".

How it works

  1. Situation: the decision and why it mattered.
  2. Task: your role and your initial position.
  3. Action – understand: listen and identify the other person's concerns.
  4. Action – resolve: shared criteria, data, POC or design doc.
  5. Result: decision, project outcome and relationship.
  6. Reflection: what you learned.

Example

STAR sample story:

  • Situation: "Two services needed asynchronous communication. I proposed Kafka; a senior colleague wanted to keep synchronous REST calls."
  • Task: "As the engineer designing the integration, I needed a decision within a sprint."
  • Action: "I asked him to explain his concerns. He was worried about operational complexity, since nobody on the team had run Kafka. We agreed criteria: reliability, operational effort and time to deliver. I built a small proof of concept and benchmark, and we compared three options in a two-page design document, including a managed queue."
  • Result: "We chose a managed queue service, which met reliability needs with little operational work, and deferred Kafka until we needed streaming. Both of us supported the decision, and he later asked me to review his designs."

Common mistakes

  • Portraying the other person negatively.
  • A story where you simply pulled rank or escalated.
  • No evidence or criteria, just persuasion.
  • Not showing what you would do if the decision went against you.
  • Choosing a trivial disagreement, such as code formatting.

How to answer in the interview

"We disagreed about whether to introduce Kafka or keep REST calls between two services. Instead of arguing, I first asked my colleague to explain his concerns, and it turned out he was worried about the operational complexity of running Kafka with no experience on the team, which was fair. We agreed on criteria, reliability, operational effort and delivery time, and I built a small proof of concept and benchmark. We compared the options in a short design document and chose a managed queue for that flow, postponing Kafka. Both of us supported the outcome and our working relationship improved. I learned that shared goals and data resolve disagreements much better than opinions."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What would you have done if the decision went against you? Disagree and commit: support it fully and raise concerns through agreed revisit criteria.
  • How do you disagree with your manager? Privately, with data, focusing on the goal, and respecting their final call.
  • What if the other person will not engage? Bring in a neutral decision owner or architecture forum.
