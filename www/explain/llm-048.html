What are the pitfalls of using an LLM as a judge, and how do you make it reliable?

LLMOps & RAG · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

LLM judges suffer from position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference and inconsistency; make them reliable with specific rubrics on binary or small scales, calibration against human labels with measured agreement, swapping order in pairwise comparisons, a strong judge that reasons before its verdict, pinned versions and human spot checks.

Detailed explanation

LLM-as-judge makes open-ended evaluation scalable, but a judge is itself a model with systematic errors.

Known biases:

  • Position bias: in pairwise comparisons the judge prefers the first (or second) answer regardless of content.
  • Verbosity bias: longer, more detailed-looking answers get higher scores even when they add nothing or contain errors.
  • Self-preference: judges tend to favour outputs from their own model family or similar style.
  • Inconsistency: the same input can get different scores across runs; and when the provider updates the judge model, scores can shift, making trends meaningless.
  • Leniency and surface cues: confident tone and formatting can mask factual errors, especially when the judge lacks the reference or source context.

Making it reliable:

  • Rubrics: ask narrow, checkable questions ("Does the answer contain any claim not supported by the context? yes/no") instead of "rate quality 1-10". Binary or 3-point scales are more consistent. Split multi-aspect quality into separate judges.
  • Give it the evidence: the reference answer, retrieved context or policy text.
  • Reasoning first: ask the judge to explain its reasoning before giving the verdict, in structured output.
  • Calibrate: label 100-200 examples by humans, measure agreement (accuracy, Cohen's kappa), refine the rubric until agreement is acceptable, and re-run calibration when the judge model changes.
  • Pairwise hygiene: run both orders (A/B and B/A) and only count consistent verdicts; treat disagreement as a tie.
  • Stability: pin judge model versions, temperature 0 where supported, multiple samples for borderline cases.
  • Humans: periodically review samples, especially failures and high-stakes categories.

How it works

  1. Define one narrow criterion per judge.
  2. Write rubric with examples of pass and fail.
  3. Human-label a calibration set; measure judge agreement.
  4. Iterate rubric; freeze judge model version.
  5. In production evals, swap positions and sample-check.
  6. Re-calibrate on judge model changes.

Example

You are grading faithfulness.
CONTEXT: {context}
ANSWER: {answer}
Step 1: List each factual claim in the ANSWER.
Step 2: For each claim, say whether the CONTEXT supports it.
Step 3: Output JSON: {"unsupported_claims": [...], "verdict": "pass" | "fail"}
Verdict is "fail" if any claim is unsupported. Length and style do not matter.

Common mistakes

  • Vague 1-10 scores with no anchors.
  • Trusting a judge never compared with humans.
  • Using the same model as generator and judge without checking self-preference.
  • Letting the judge model float to "latest".

How to answer in the interview

"LLM judges are scalable but biased: they favour a position in pairwise comparisons, prefer longer answers, prefer their own model family's style, and can be inconsistent or shift when the provider updates the model. I make them reliable with narrow rubrics on binary or small scales, giving the judge the reference or context, and asking for reasoning before the verdict. I calibrate against human-labelled examples and measure agreement before trusting it, run pairwise comparisons in both orders, pin the judge version, and keep humans reviewing samples for critical metrics."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Detecting judge drift? Re-score a fixed calibration set regularly; alert on agreement changes.
  • Which judge model? A strong model, ideally from a different family than the generator.
  • Cheaper judges? Small fine-tuned classifiers for specific checks once calibrated.
