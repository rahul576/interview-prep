How do you estimate work and agree on delivery timelines?

Agile & Scrum · scenario · levels mid

In one line

I break work into small pieces, let the people doing it estimate relatively, use spikes for unknowns, include testing, review, deployment and risk, forecast from historical velocity or throughput, and communicate a range with confidence rather than a single date, then agree scope trade-offs and update early.

Detailed explanation

Estimates are forecasts under uncertainty, not promises. The two classic failures are optimism (estimating only the coding) and false precision (one exact date with no confidence). A good process reduces uncertainty and makes it visible.

First, decompose: large items hide work, while small stories are easier to understand and estimate. The people doing the work estimate, usually relatively with story points and planning poker; when two developers give a 2 and an 8, the conversation uncovers different assumptions. Spikes time-box research into unknowns, such as a new library or vendor API, before estimating.

Second, count all the work: tests, code review, documentation, deployment, coordination with other teams, data migration, security review and, often forgotten, waiting time. Add a buffer for known risks.

Third, forecast from data. Velocity (points per sprint) or throughput (items finished per week) from recent history is more honest than gut feel. Teams using flow metrics can run a Monte Carlo simulation on past throughput to produce statements like "85 percent likely by 14 March". The cone of uncertainty says early estimates are wide and narrow as work progresses, so communicating a range is accurate, not evasive.

Fourth, agree with stakeholders. If the date is fixed, scope is the variable: define a minimum viable scope and order the rest by value. Track progress (burnup charts help) and raise deviations as soon as they appear, not at the deadline.

How it works

  1. Split the work into small stories with acceptance criteria.
  2. Spike the unknowns; estimate with planning poker.
  3. Include testing, review, deployment, dependencies and risk buffer.
  4. Forecast with historical velocity or throughput; give a range and confidence.
  5. Agree a date or scope with the product owner, with MVP scope first.
  6. Track with a burnup chart and re-forecast every sprint.

Example

Feature: bulk invoice upload
Backlog after refinement: 42 points (incl. 5-pt spike done)
Velocity last 6 sprints: 18, 22, 20, 15, 21, 19  -> avg 19, range 15-22

Forecast: 42 / 22 = 1.9 sprints (best)   42 / 15 = 2.8 sprints (worst)
Message: "Likely end of sprint 3 (two weeks' buffer), at worst mid sprint 4.
          Risk: bank file format sign-off from Finance by 10 June.
          MVP (CSV only, 28 pts) is safe for sprint 2 if needed."

Common mistakes

  • Estimating only coding time.
  • Giving a single date under pressure without stating assumptions and risks.
  • Converting story points into hours or comparing velocity across teams.
  • Hiding slippage until the deadline.
  • Managers estimating on behalf of the team.

How to answer in the interview

"I break the work into small stories and have the people doing it estimate relatively with planning poker, which surfaces different assumptions. Unknowns get a time-boxed spike first. I make sure the estimate includes testing, review, deployment and coordination, not just coding. Then I forecast from our historical velocity or throughput and give a range with confidence, for example 'likely three sprints, at worst four', plus the main risks. If the date is fixed, I agree with the product owner on scope trade-offs, delivering a minimum viable scope first. And I track progress every sprint and flag any deviation early rather than at the deadline."

Likely follow-up questions

  • A stakeholder pushes for an unrealistic date? Show the data and options: reduce scope, add time, or accept risk, and let them choose explicitly.
  • Story points or hours? Points for relative sizing and forecasting; hours only for short-term task planning if at all.
  • What is #NoEstimates? Forecasting from throughput of similarly sized items instead of estimating each one.
