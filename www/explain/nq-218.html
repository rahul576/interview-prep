What are the benefits of breaking a monolithic application into microservices?

Microservices · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

Splitting a monolith gives independent, faster deployments, independent scaling of hot parts, fault isolation, team autonomy and smaller codebases, but only pays off if splits follow clear domain boundaries and the organisation can absorb the distributed complexity.

Detailed explanation

A large monolith often suffers from release coupling: dozens of developers merge into one codebase, every release ships everything, and one risky change delays all others. Builds and test suites take an hour; nobody understands the whole system.

Independent deployment and faster releases. Each extracted service has its own pipeline, so a team deploys small changes many times a day without a release train. Smaller changes are less risky and easier to roll back.

Independent scaling. Different parts have different load. Search or checkout may need twenty instances during a sale, while admin needs two. With services you scale only what is hot, often automatically with Kubernetes autoscaling, and can choose fitting resources (memory-heavy for search, CPU for image processing).

Fault isolation. A memory leak in the recommendation service crashes only that service; with timeouts, circuit breakers and fallbacks, the shop keeps selling. In a monolith, one leak takes down everything.

Team autonomy and technology. Teams own services end to end, aligned with business domains, and make their own decisions about libraries, upgrades (moving one service to a new Java LTS at a time) or even languages and databases.

Maintainability. Smaller codebases are easier to understand, onboard onto and test.

The trade-offs are real: network calls instead of method calls, eventual consistency and sagas instead of transactions, distributed tracing and centralised logging, contract testing, more infrastructure, and a need for DevOps maturity. Splitting along the wrong lines creates a distributed monolith, where services must still be deployed together. So splits should follow bounded contexts, usually extracted gradually with the strangler fig pattern.

How it works

  1. Map the domain and find seams (bounded contexts).
  2. Pick a module with clear boundaries and a strong reason (scale, release pace).
  3. Build the new service; route that functionality through a gateway or proxy (strangler fig).
  4. Migrate its data into its own database; sync during transition.
  5. Retire the monolith code; repeat.

Example

Before: shop-monolith.war (catalogue, cart, checkout, payments, admin) – 1 release per fortnight
After step 1:
  /api/search/**  -> search-service   (12 pods, OpenSearch, deploys daily)
  everything else -> shop-monolith    (3 pods)
Result: search scales during sales independently; search team ships without the release train.

Common mistakes

  • Big-bang rewrites instead of incremental extraction.
  • Splitting but keeping a shared database.
  • Expecting benefits without investing in CI/CD and observability.
  • Extracting too many tiny services at once.

How to answer in the interview

"Breaking up a large monolith lets each service be built, tested and deployed independently, so teams release more often with smaller, less risky changes. You can scale just the hot parts, such as search or checkout, instead of the whole app. Failures are better isolated: a bug in recommendations need not take down payments, given proper timeouts and circuit breakers. Teams own services end to end and can upgrade at their own pace, and codebases stay smaller. The trade-offs are network calls, eventual consistency, tracing and more infrastructure, so I split along clear domain boundaries and extract gradually with the strangler fig pattern."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What signs show a monolith is ready to be split? Teams blocking each other's releases, very different scaling needs, long builds, stable domain boundaries.
  • What is the strangler fig pattern? Gradually routing functionality to new services until the old system can be removed.
  • Which module do you extract first? One with clear boundaries, low coupling and a strong business reason.
