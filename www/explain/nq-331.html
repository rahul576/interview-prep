What is t-shirt sizing, and how is it used for estimation?

Agile & Scrum · concept · levels entry

In one line

T-shirt sizing is a fast relative estimation technique where items are sized XS, S, M, L or XL instead of hours or points, used early for epics, roadmaps and quarterly planning when detail is unknown; sizes are often mapped to rough ranges, and anything XL gets split or spiked.

Detailed explanation

Early in planning, requirements are fuzzy, so asking for "how many days" invites false precision: a number that looks exact but hides huge uncertainty. People are much better at comparing than at estimating absolute effort, which is why relative estimation works. T-shirt sizes are the coarsest and fastest form: "this is bigger than the search epic but smaller than the payments migration".

The process is lightweight. The team picks a few reference items that everyone remembers, such as "the CSV export was an S, the SSO integration was an L", and compares new items with them. Silent sorting or affinity estimation lets a team size dozens of items in an hour. The value is not just the size: the discussion surfaces assumptions, unknowns and dependencies, for example "this is only an M if the vendor API supports webhooks".

For capacity planning, sizes are usually mapped to rough ranges agreed within the team, for example S is a few days, M around one sprint, L two to three sprints, and XL more than a quarter. Some organisations map them to story point ranges. An XL is a signal of too much uncertainty: it should be split into smaller epics or investigated with a spike before anyone commits.

T-shirt sizes are for planning horizons, not sprint commitments. When an item approaches implementation, it is refined into stories and estimated in story points or by count.

How it works

  1. Agree the size scale and a few reference items per size.
  2. Product owner presents each item briefly; the team asks questions.
  3. Each person suggests a size (or the team sorts cards into columns).
  4. Discuss differences, especially extremes; capture assumptions and risks.
  5. Map sizes to rough ranges to check against capacity.
  6. Split or spike XL items; refine into stories closer to delivery.

Example

Common mistakes

  • Treating sizes as commitments or converting them back into exact days.
  • No reference items, so "M" means different things to different people.
  • Committing to XL items without splitting them.
  • Letting one senior person size everything alone, losing the team's insight.

How to answer in the interview

"T-shirt sizing is a relative estimation technique where we size items as XS, S, M, L or XL instead of hours or points. We use it early, for epics and roadmap items in quarterly planning, when details are unknown and precise numbers would be false precision. The team compares items with reference items it knows, and the discussion is valuable because it surfaces assumptions and risks. We map sizes to rough ranges, for example M is about a sprint and L two to three sprints, so we can check capacity. Anything XL is too uncertain, so we split it or do a spike first."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How does it differ from story points? It is coarser and used for longer-range planning; points are for refined stories and velocity.
  • What is a spike? A time-boxed investigation to reduce uncertainty before estimating.
  • How do you handle disagreement in sizes? Let the highest and lowest explain their reasoning, then re-vote.
