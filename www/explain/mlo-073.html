What does the EU AI Act mean for teams that build and operate ML systems?

MLOps · concept · levels senior, lead

In one line

The EU AI Act is a risk-based regulation: some practices are prohibited, high-risk systems must meet strict requirements (risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness), general-purpose AI providers have their own duties, and obligations phase in through 2026-2027; good MLOps produces much of the evidence.

Detailed explanation

The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Its categories:

  • Prohibited practices (applying since February 2025): for example social scoring, manipulative techniques exploiting vulnerabilities, untargeted scraping for facial recognition databases and most emotion recognition at work or school.
  • High-risk systems: AI used as safety components of regulated products, or in areas listed in Annex III such as employment and hiring, access to education, creditworthiness, essential services, critical infrastructure, law enforcement and migration.
  • Limited risk / transparency: chatbots must disclose they are AI; deepfakes and certain generated content must be labelled.
  • Minimal risk: most other systems, with voluntary codes.
  • General-purpose AI (GPAI) models: providers must supply technical documentation, copyright policies and training data summaries since August 2025, with extra duties for models with systemic risk.

For high-risk systems, providers need a risk management system, data governance (relevant, representative training data, bias examination), technical documentation, automatic logging of events, transparency instructions for deployers, human oversight, appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, a quality management system, conformity assessment and registration before placing on the market, and post-market monitoring with serious-incident reporting. Deployers must use systems as instructed, ensure human oversight, keep logs and, in some cases, run a fundamental-rights impact assessment.

Most high-risk obligations were scheduled for August 2026 (some product-related ones August 2027), and the EU has proposed adjusting parts of the timeline, so teams should check the current dates with legal rather than wait.

How it works

  1. Inventory all AI systems and their use cases.
  2. Classify each with legal: prohibited, high-risk, transparency, minimal; and your role (provider or deployer).
  3. Map requirements to platform capabilities.
  4. Close gaps: lineage, documentation, bias testing, logging, oversight, monitoring.
  5. Produce evidence automatically from pipelines.
  6. Set up post-market monitoring and incident reporting.

Example

system: credit-limit-model
classification: high-risk (Annex III, creditworthiness)
role: provider
evidence:
  data_governance: datasets/credit/v12 lineage + bias_report_2026-10.html
  technical_docs: model_card_v7.md
  logging: prediction logs, 10-year retention, request_id + model_version
  human_oversight: review queue for declines above limit; override audit
  robustness: stress tests, adversarial input suite in CI
  post_market: drift + fairness monitors, incident runbook

Common mistakes

  • Assuming only "AI companies" are affected; deployers have obligations too.
  • Treating compliance as a one-off document instead of continuous evidence.
  • Waiting for final guidance before building lineage and logging.
  • Forgetting chatbot transparency duties.

How to answer in the interview

"The AI Act is risk-based. Some uses are banned, like social scoring. High-risk systems, such as credit scoring or hiring, need risk management, data governance and bias checks, technical documentation, automatic logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, a conformity assessment and post-market monitoring. GPAI providers have their own duties, and chatbots need transparency. Obligations phase in, with most high-risk ones from 2026, so I'd start now. For MLOps teams the good news is that lineage, reproducibility, model cards, evaluation and bias reports, prediction logs and monitoring are exactly the evidence needed. I'd involve legal early to classify each system."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What's hardest to retrofit? Lineage and data governance for old models: proving what data trained them and how it was checked.
  • Provider versus deployer? The provider develops and places the system on the market; the deployer uses it under its authority. Each has different duties.
  • Does it apply outside the EU? Yes, if the system is placed on the EU market or its output is used in the EU.
