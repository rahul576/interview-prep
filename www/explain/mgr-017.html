How do you keep an on-call rotation sustainable and prevent burnout?

People Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Keep on-call sustainable with enough people in the rotation (or follow-the-sun), actionable alerts only, runbooks and training, compensation and recovery time, and by tracking pages and fixing recurring causes.

Detailed explanation

On-call burnout is one of the main reasons engineers leave operations-heavy teams. It comes from a combination of frequency (being on call too often), interruptions (too many pages, especially at night), stress (not knowing what to do) and lack of recognition. A sustainable rotation addresses all four.

Rotation size: with fewer than about five or six people, each engineer is on call too often. Six to eight per rotation is a common target, and where teams span time zones, follow-the-sun lets each region cover its own daytime hours, removing most night pages. Small teams can share a rotation with a sister team.

Alert quality is the biggest lever. Every page should be urgent, actionable and user-impacting. Alert on symptoms linked to SLOs (error rate, latency, error budget burn rate) rather than on every CPU spike. Noisy alerts are tuned, downgraded to tickets or deleted. Google's SRE guidance suggests a small number of incidents per shift (around two per 12-hour shift) so each can be handled properly and followed up.

Preparation: runbooks for each alert, shadowing before going primary, and clear escalation paths reduce fear. Recognition: on-call pay or time off after heavy nights, and no expectation of a full workday after being woken repeatedly. Finally, measure: pages per shift, out-of-hours pages, time to acknowledge, and repeat offenders. Reserve time each sprint to fix the root causes and toil.

How it works

  1. Size the rotation (six to eight) or use follow-the-sun across regions.
  2. Audit alerts: keep only actionable, SLO-based pages.
  3. Write runbooks and require shadow shifts before primary on-call.
  4. Define escalation paths and a secondary on-call.
  5. Compensate on-call and give recovery time after heavy incidents.
  6. Review on-call metrics weekly and fix top recurring alerts.

Example

A burn-rate alert that pages only when the SLO is truly at risk (Prometheus):

- alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
  expr: |
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[1h]))
     / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
  for: 5m
  labels: {severity: page}
  annotations:
    runbook: https://wiki.example.com/runbooks/checkout-errors

Common mistakes

  • Paging on causes (CPU, disk at 70 percent) instead of user symptoms.
  • Rotations of two or three people.
  • No runbooks, so every page is a fresh investigation.
  • Not tracking on-call load, so nobody sees it getting worse.
  • Expecting full productivity the day after a bad night.

How to answer in the interview

"First I make sure the rotation has enough people, ideally six to eight, and use follow-the-sun where we have several time zones so nobody is woken every night. The biggest lever is noise: every page must be actionable and tied to user impact, ideally SLO burn-rate alerts, and anything else becomes a ticket or is deleted. Runbooks, shadow shifts and clear escalation make on-call less stressful for newer engineers. People get compensation or time off after heavy nights. And I track pages per shift and out-of-hours pages, reviewing them weekly, so the team fixes recurring issues and on-call gets better over time instead of worse."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What is a reasonable maximum number of pages per shift? Around two incidents per 12-hour shift; more means alerts or reliability need work.
  • How do you handle a team too small for a healthy rotation? Merge with a sister team, use business-hours-only paging for lower tiers, or follow-the-sun.
  • What do you do with an alert that fires often but is never actionable? Remove it or convert it to a ticket or dashboard.
