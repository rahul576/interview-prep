What is model calibration, and when does it matter?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

A model is calibrated when its predicted probabilities match observed frequencies; measure it with reliability diagrams, ECE or the Brier score, fix it with Platt scaling, isotonic regression or temperature scaling, and care when probabilities drive decisions.

Detailed explanation

If a model says 0.8 for a thousand cases, about 800 should be positive. Discrimination (ranking positives above negatives, measured by AUC) and calibration are different properties: a model can rank perfectly yet output 0.99 for everything positive-ish. Calibration is about whether the numbers can be used as probabilities.

Common causes of poor calibration: boosted trees and modern deep networks are often overconfident; regularisation and early stopping shift scores; resampling or class weights for imbalance inflate the positive rate; and prior shift in production (the base rate changes) breaks calibration even when ranking still works.

Measuring: a reliability diagram bins predictions (say ten bins) and plots mean predicted probability against observed positive rate; a perfect model lies on the diagonal. Expected calibration error (ECE) is the weighted average gap across bins. The Brier score (mean squared error of probabilities) combines calibration and discrimination. Log loss also penalises miscalibration.

Fixing: post-hoc calibration fitted on a held-out set: Platt scaling (logistic regression on the score, good for small data and sigmoid-shaped distortion), isotonic regression (non-parametric, flexible, needs more data), and temperature scaling for neural networks. In scikit-learn, CalibratedClassifierCV does this with cross-validation.

When it matters: expected value calculations (expected loss = probability times amount), insurance and credit pricing, combining models, thresholds for human review, risk communication, and anything consuming the score as a probability. If you only rank (top-N recommendations), it matters less.

How it works

  1. Hold out a calibration set not used for training.
  2. Plot the reliability diagram and compute ECE and Brier.
  3. Fit Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling on the holdout.
  4. Validate on a separate test set.
  5. Ship the calibrator with the model as one artefact.
  6. Monitor calibration in production as labels arrive.

Example

from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV, calibration_curve
from sklearn.metrics import brier_score_loss
cal = CalibratedClassifierCV(base_model, method="isotonic", cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
p = cal.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
frac_pos, mean_pred = calibration_curve(y_test, p, n_bins=10)
print("Brier:", brier_score_loss(y_test, p))

Common mistakes

  • Fitting the calibrator on training data.
  • Undersampling for imbalance and using the scores as real probabilities.
  • Assuming good AUC means good probabilities.
  • Not monitoring calibration after a base-rate shift.

How to answer in the interview

"Calibration means predicted probabilities match reality: among cases scored 0.8, about 80 percent are positive. I check it with a reliability diagram and expected calibration error or the Brier score. Many models are overconfident, and resampling distorts probabilities, so I fix it post-training with Platt scaling, isotonic regression or temperature scaling on a held-out set. It matters whenever probabilities drive decisions, like expected loss, pricing, combining models or human-review thresholds; for pure ranking it matters less. I monitor it because drift breaks calibration."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Good AUC, poor calibration? AUC only measures ranking; any monotonic distortion of scores leaves AUC unchanged but ruins calibration.
  • Platt or isotonic? Platt for small data or sigmoid distortion; isotonic for larger data and arbitrary shapes.
  • How do you fix calibration after a base-rate shift? Recalibrate on recent labelled data or adjust the prior.
