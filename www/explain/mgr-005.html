Two senior engineers on your team strongly disagree on an architecture decision and the conflict is affecting the team. What do you do?

People Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Talk to each separately, bring them back to shared goals and explicit criteria, use a design doc or time-boxed POC, let a clear decision owner decide and record it as an ADR, expect disagree and commit, and address behaviour separately.

Detailed explanation

Technical disagreement between senior engineers is healthy; it often leads to better designs. The problem is when it becomes personal, stalls decisions or affects the rest of the team, for example through tense meetings, blocked pull requests or people taking sides. A manager's job is to keep the debate about ideas and turn it into a decision with a clear process.

Start with separate conversations. Each person explains their position and concerns without an audience, which lowers defensiveness and often reveals hidden drivers such as fear of on-call burden or a past bad experience. Make it clear that disagreement is welcome, but the impact on the team is not acceptable.

Then bring them together around shared goals and explicit decision criteria: scalability, delivery time, operational cost, security, team skills and reversibility. Ask for a short design document comparing options against those criteria, and if facts are missing, a time-boxed proof of concept or benchmark. Classify the decision: a reversible "two-way door" can be decided quickly; an irreversible one deserves more rigour.

A named decision owner, often the tech lead or architect, makes the call, and the decision and reasoning are recorded in an Architecture Decision Record (ADR). Both engineers are expected to disagree and commit. If disrespectful behaviour continues, handle it directly as a performance and conduct issue.

How it works

  1. Hold separate one-on-ones to understand positions and concerns.
  2. Set expectations: debate ideas, respect people.
  3. Agree shared goals and weighted decision criteria together.
  4. Produce a design doc; run a POC if facts are missing.
  5. Decision owner decides; document in an ADR with revisit conditions.
  6. Follow up on behaviour and team health.

Example

A lightweight ADR template:

# ADR-014: Event bus for order processing
Status: Accepted
Context: Order service needs async integration with billing and shipping.
Options: (A) Kafka, (B) Azure Service Bus
Criteria: throughput, ordering, ops effort, team skills, cost
Decision: Service Bus with sessions for per-order ordering.
Consequences: Lower ops effort; revisit if throughput > 5k msg/s.
Dissent noted: Kafka preferred by R. for replay; replay covered by archive.

Common mistakes

  • Letting the conflict run, hoping they sort it out.
  • Picking a side based on seniority or friendship.
  • Endless meetings without a decision owner or deadline.
  • Not documenting the reasoning, so the debate resurfaces.
  • Ignoring disrespectful behaviour because the person is technically strong.

How to answer in the interview

"I would first talk to each engineer separately to understand their position and concerns, and make clear that disagreement is healthy but its impact on the team is not. Then I would bring them together and steer the discussion towards shared goals and explicit criteria, like scalability, delivery time, operational cost and team skills. I would ask for a short design doc comparing the options and, if needed, a time-boxed proof of concept. A clear decision owner then decides, we record it in an ADR including the dissent, and both are expected to disagree and commit. If behaviour such as disrespect in reviews continues, I address it directly as a performance matter."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you prevent the "loser" from feeling sidelined? Credit their input publicly, record their concerns in the ADR and give them ownership of a related part.
  • What if you are the decision owner and lack the expertise? Bring in a neutral expert or architecture forum, and decide on the agreed criteria.
  • When would you revisit the decision? When the revisit conditions in the ADR are met, such as load thresholds or new facts.
