Which join is appropriate for retrieving matching customer and order records?

Databases & SQL · concept · levels entry

In one line

Use an INNER JOIN between customers and orders when you only need customers who actually have orders (for example, revenue per customer per year); switch to a LEFT JOIN only if customers with zero orders must also appear.

Detailed explanation

A join combines rows from two tables using a join condition, here c.customer_id = o.customer_id. An INNER JOIN keeps only the pairs where the condition is true, so every output row is a real customer–order combination. For a revenue report that is exactly right: a customer with no orders has no revenue, so leaving them out changes nothing in the totals.

The choice changes when the business question changes. If the report must list every customer, including those with zero revenue, you need customers LEFT JOIN orders. Unmatched customers then come back with NULL order columns, so you wrap the total in COALESCE(SUM(o.amount), 0). There is a catch: the year comes from o.order_date, which is NULL for customers without orders. To show "zero revenue in 2024" you need a year list (a calendar table or a generated series) cross-joined with customers, then left-joined to orders.

Also think about cardinality. Customer-to-orders is one-to-many, so the join repeats customer columns once per order. That is fine before GROUP BY, but joining a second one-to-many table such as order items will multiply rows and inflate sums.

How it works

  1. The database reads customers and orders (the optimiser picks hash, merge or nested-loop join).
  2. For each customer it finds orders whose customer_id matches.
  3. INNER JOIN outputs only matched pairs; LEFT JOIN also outputs unmatched customers with NULLs.
  4. WHERE filters the joined rows, then GROUP BY and SUM aggregate them.
  5. An index on orders(customer_id) or orders(customer_id, order_date) speeds this up.

Example

SELECT c.customer_id, c.name,
       EXTRACT(YEAR FROM o.order_date) AS order_year,
       SUM(o.amount) AS total_revenue
FROM customers c
INNER JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'COMPLETED'
GROUP BY c.customer_id, c.name, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM o.order_date)
ORDER BY c.name, order_year;

Common mistakes

  • Using LEFT JOIN "to be safe" and then filtering o.status in WHERE, which silently turns it back into an inner join.
  • Forgetting that LEFT JOIN gives NULL sums, not zero, without COALESCE.
  • Joining order items as well and double-counting order amounts.
  • Joining on customer name instead of the key.

How to answer in the interview

"I'd use an INNER JOIN on customer_id, because the report is revenue from orders, and a customer without orders contributes nothing, so only matching rows are needed. If the business wants every customer listed, even with zero revenue, I'd switch to customers LEFT JOIN orders with COALESCE on the sum, and I'd mention that the year then has to come from a calendar or year list, since unmatched customers have no order date. I'd also check that no other one-to-many join inflates the totals."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How does WHERE behave differently with a LEFT JOIN? A WHERE condition on right-table columns removes the NULL rows; put it in the ON clause to keep unmatched customers.
  • Which index helps this query? An index on orders(customer_id, order_date), plus the customer primary key.
  • What does a RIGHT JOIN do? The mirror of LEFT JOIN; most teams rewrite it as a LEFT JOIN for readability.
