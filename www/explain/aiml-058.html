How do you keep your AI knowledge up to date, and how do you evaluate new AI claims critically?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

I stay current by following primary sources and experimenting hands-on, and I evaluate claims critically by asking what was measured, against what baseline, and then testing on my own use cases with a small evaluation set, while investing in fundamentals that last.

Detailed explanation

The interviewer is checking two things: that you learn continuously in a fast-moving field, and that you have the judgement not to be carried away by hype. A strong answer names concrete habits rather than "I read a lot".

Primary sources beat summaries: provider release notes and model cards, official documentation, specifications such as the Model Context Protocol, engineering blogs from teams running AI in production, and a few well-chosen papers. Social media is useful for discovery, but claims should be traced back to the source.

Hands-on experimentation is the fastest teacher. An hour building a small agent, trying a new coding assistant on a real ticket or wiring up a retrieval pipeline reveals limitations that no announcement mentions.

Critical evaluation means treating benchmarks and marketing as hypotheses. Questions to ask: which tasks and dataset; was the test set possibly in the training data (contamination); what was the baseline and the prompt; were results independently reproduced; what are the cost and latency; and does the benchmark resemble my workload at all? A model that tops a leaderboard may still be worse for your domain, your language or your latency budget.

The most reliable check is a personal or team evaluation set: 20-100 representative tasks with expected outcomes, rerun whenever a new model or tool appears. Communities, colleagues and meetups add practical experience from production. Finally, fundamentals such as evaluation, retrieval, security, prompt injection and system design keep their value while specific tools change.

How it works

  1. Curate a short list of primary sources and review them weekly.
  2. Turn interesting announcements into a time-boxed experiment.
  3. Question every claim: task, data, baseline, reproducibility, cost.
  4. Rerun your own evaluation set on the new model or tool.
  5. Share findings with the team (a short write-up or demo).
  6. Keep deepening fundamentals alongside tool knowledge.

Example

Sample STAR story:

  • Situation: a new model release claimed large gains on coding benchmarks, and the team wanted to switch our PR-summary bot immediately.
  • Task: decide with evidence rather than hype.
  • Action: I reran our 60-case evaluation set of real pull requests, comparing accuracy of risk notes, latency and cost per summary against the current model.
  • Result: quality improved slightly on large diffs but cost doubled; we routed only large diffs to the new model and saved about 40% compared with switching everything.

Common mistakes

  • Listing only social media or newsletters as sources.
  • Accepting benchmark charts at face value.
  • Chasing every new tool without going deep on any.
  • Having no way to compare models on your own tasks.

How to answer in the interview

"The field moves weekly, so I keep a routine. I follow primary sources: release notes, documentation, specs like MCP and a few engineering blogs and papers, rather than relying on summaries. I try things hands-on, because an hour of building teaches more than ten articles. When I see a big claim, I ask what was measured, on which tasks, against which baseline, and whether anyone reproduced it. Then I test it on our own evaluation set. Recently that showed a hyped model was better but twice as expensive, so we used it only where it mattered. And I keep investing in fundamentals like evaluation, retrieval and security."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which recent development matters most for DevOps? Agentic tools connected through standards like MCP, because they can act on infrastructure and need strong guardrails.
  • How big should a personal eval set be? Start with 20-50 real cases; grow it whenever you find a failure.
  • How do you spot benchmark contamination? Be wary of public test sets and prefer fresh, private tasks.
