How do you decide how much autonomy to give an agent?

AI Agents & AIOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Decide autonomy per action, not per agent, using levels: suggest → act with approval → act and notify → fully autonomous, based on risk and reversibility, proven reliability in evals and supervised production, cost of errors versus value of speed, and regulatory and accountability requirements, increasing gradually based on data and reducing it when error rates rise.

Detailed explanation

"How autonomous should the agent be?" is the wrong granularity. One agent may safely read logs on its own but must never delete a database without approval. So autonomy is set per action type.

Levels give a shared vocabulary:

  1. Suggest: the agent drafts, and a human performs the action (a reply draft, a proposed config change).
  2. Act with approval: the agent prepares the exact action; a human approves before execution.
  3. Act and notify: the agent acts and informs a human who can review and undo.
  4. Fully autonomous: the agent acts; humans review only aggregate metrics and samples.

Risk and reversibility are the primary factors. Reading data, labelling tickets or creating drafts are low risk and easy to undo. Sending money, emailing customers, changing production or deleting data are high risk and often irreversible. Blast radius matters too: one record versus thousands.

Proven reliability: an action type earns a higher level through evidence: high success rates in evaluations, then in supervised production where humans approve or review, measured by approval rates without edits, error rates and incidents. For example, if 98 percent of proposed refunds under 50 euros were approved unchanged over 2,000 cases, auto-approving that class becomes defensible.

Cost versus value: compare the expected cost of errors with the value of speed and saved human effort. Fast autonomous remediation of a common incident at 3 a.m. may be worth a small risk; a rare high-value transfer is not.

Regulation and accountability: some decisions legally require human involvement, for example GDPR Article 22 on solely automated decisions with significant effects, and the EU AI Act's human-oversight requirements for high-risk systems. Someone must remain accountable.

Autonomy is dynamic: it increases gradually with data and can be reduced automatically when error rates rise or after a model change.

How it works

  1. Inventory the agent's actions; rate risk, reversibility and blast radius.
  2. Assign each action a starting level, usually conservative.
  3. Collect evidence: eval results, approval-without-edit rates, errors.
  4. Define promotion criteria and thresholds per action.
  5. Promote gradually; monitor and keep undo paths.
  6. Demote automatically on error spikes, incidents or model changes.

Example

autonomy_policy:
  read_logs:              { level: autonomous }
  add_ticket_label:       { level: act_and_notify }
  restart_pod_staging:    { level: autonomous }
  restart_pod_prod:       { level: act_with_approval,
                            promote_if: { approvals_unedited_rate: ">=0.98", min_cases: 500, incidents: 0 } }
  refund:
    - when: "amount <= 50"
      level: act_and_notify
    - when: "amount > 50"
      level: act_with_approval
  delete_customer_data:   { level: suggest }    # legal review required
  demote_if: { error_rate_7d: ">0.02", or: "model_version_changed" }

Common mistakes

  • One autonomy setting for the whole agent.
  • Granting autonomy based on demos rather than data.
  • Ignoring blast radius and reversibility.
  • No path to reduce autonomy after a regression.
  • Forgetting legal requirements for human decision-making.

How to answer in the interview

"I think of autonomy as levels and choose it per action rather than per agent: suggest, act with approval, act and notify, and fully autonomous. The main factors are risk and reversibility: reading data or creating a draft is low risk, while sending money, emailing customers or changing production is high risk and often irreversible. Then proven reliability: an action type that succeeds consistently in evals and in supervised production, for example approved unchanged in ninety-eight percent of hundreds of cases, can move up a level. I weigh the cost of errors against the value of speed, and consider regulations, since some decisions legally require a human. Autonomy increases gradually based on data and can be reduced again if error rates rise."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Data to move an action from approval to autonomous? A large sample of approvals without edits, low error and incident rates, and reliable undo.
  • Who decides autonomy levels? The business owner of the process with engineering, security and, where relevant, legal.
  • What triggers demotion? Error spikes, incidents, model or prompt changes and new regulations.
