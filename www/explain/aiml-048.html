What intellectual property and copyright issues should teams consider with generative AI?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Generative AI raises separate IP questions about training data (legally contested), outputs that may resemble protected works, ownership of AI-generated content (varies by jurisdiction) and code matching open-source licences; manage them with enterprise terms and indemnities, policies, filters, human review and legal involvement for high-risk uses.

Detailed explanation

Training data. Whether training models on copyrighted text, images, music or code without permission is lawful is being argued in courts and legislatures in several countries, with concepts like fair use in the US and text-and-data-mining exceptions with opt-outs in the EU. Outcomes differ by jurisdiction and are still developing, so avoid stating them as settled. This matters most if your team trains or fine-tunes models: know the provenance and licence of your data, respect opt-outs and keep records.

Outputs. Generated content can sometimes closely resemble or reproduce protected works, recognisable characters, logos, brands or an artist's distinctive work, or reproduce long passages of text. Publishing such content could infringe copyright or trademark regardless of how it was made. Prompts that ask for "in the style of" a living artist or a famous character carry higher risk.

Ownership. In many jurisdictions, purely AI-generated material may not receive copyright protection the way human-authored work does; meaningful human creative contribution matters. The US Copyright Office, for instance, has required human authorship. This affects whether a company can protect logos, product designs or marketing assets created mainly by AI.

Code. Coding assistants can occasionally suggest code that matches existing open-source code under licences such as GPL with obligations. Many tools provide filters that block or flag matches with public code and reference tracking.

Practical controls: use enterprise versions of tools whose providers offer clear terms on data use and, in some cases, IP indemnities (check the conditions, which often require filters to be enabled); write a company AI usage policy covering permitted uses, confidential inputs and publication; enable code-matching filters and run licence scanning in CI; require human review and editing of anything published; keep records of prompts and edits for key assets; and involve legal for brand, advertising and product-design uses.

How it works

  1. Classify use cases by IP risk (internal drafts vs published marketing vs product code).
  2. Choose enterprise tools with suitable terms and indemnities.
  3. Set policy: what may be generated, what inputs are allowed, who approves publication.
  4. Enable filters (public-code matching, brand and character restrictions) and licence scanning.
  5. Review outputs with humans and legal for high-risk cases; keep records.

Example

# Excerpt from an internal generative AI policy
allowed_tools: [enterprise-chat, enterprise-code-assistant, approved-image-generator]
code:
  public_code_filter: required
  licence_scan_in_ci: required
marketing_images:
  prohibited_prompts: ["real people", "competitor brands", "named living artists' styles"]
  review: brand team + legal before publication
records: keep prompt, model, date and human edits for published assets

Common mistakes

  • Assuming anything generated is free to use commercially.
  • Ignoring licence terms of the AI tool itself.
  • Relying on an indemnity without meeting its conditions.
  • Publishing AI images featuring recognisable characters or brands.

How to answer in the interview

"There are separate questions. Training on copyrighted data is being contested in courts and legislation, which matters if we fine-tune models ourselves. Outputs can sometimes resemble protected works, characters or brands, which is a risk when publishing. Ownership of purely AI-generated content may be weaker in many jurisdictions. And code suggestions can match open-source code with licence obligations. In practice I'd use enterprise tools with clear terms and indemnities, set a usage policy, enable public-code filters and licence scanning, keep humans reviewing what's published, and involve legal for high-risk uses."

Likely follow-up questions

  • AI-generated marketing images in a brand-sensitive company? Approved tools only, restricted prompts, brand and legal review, and records of creation.
  • What does a public-code filter do? Blocks or flags suggestions that match public repositories, often with licence references.
  • Is fine-tuning on customer data an IP issue? Yes, check contracts and consent for that data.
