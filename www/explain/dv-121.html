What is DevOps, and what are its core principles?

DevOps & CI/CD · concept · levels entry

In one line

DevOps is a culture and set of practices that unites development and operations so one team can deliver changes quickly and reliably, built on automation, small frequent releases with fast feedback, shared ownership ("you build it, you run it") and measurement such as the DORA metrics.

Detailed explanation

Traditionally developers "threw code over the wall" to operations. Developers were rewarded for change, operations for stability, so releases were rare, large and risky, and incidents became blame games. DevOps removes that wall: one team owns a service from commit to production.

It is not a job title or a tool. CALMS summarises it: Culture (collaboration, blameless postmortems), Automation (CI/CD, infrastructure as code, automated tests), Lean (small batches, less waste), Measurement (monitoring, SLOs, delivery metrics) and Sharing (knowledge, tools, on-call).

Small, frequent releases are central: a fifty-line change is easy to test, deploy and roll back; a quarterly release of hundreds of changes is hard to debug. Feedback comes from CI, production monitoring and users.

The DORA metrics are deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and time to restore service (now called failed deployment recovery time). The research shows speed and stability improve together. Platform engineering and SRE extend these ideas with self-service golden paths and SLO-driven reliability.

How it works

  1. Teams own services end to end, including on-call.
  2. Every commit goes through automated build, test and security checks.
  3. Infrastructure and configuration are defined as code and reviewed.
  4. Changes ship in small batches with automated deployment and rollback.
  5. Monitoring and SLOs give feedback; incidents get blameless postmortems.
  6. DORA metrics track improvement over time.

Example

A team deploys monthly with a weekend release window and frequent rollbacks. Steps taken: trunk-based development with feature flags, a CI pipeline under ten minutes, Terraform for environments, canary deployments with automatic rollback, and dashboards per service. Six months later they deploy several times a day, lead time drops from three weeks to under a day, and change failure rate falls because each change is small.

DORA snapshot          Before      After
Deployment frequency   Monthly     Several/day
Lead time for changes  ~3 weeks    < 1 day
Change failure rate    ~30%        ~8%
Time to restore        Hours       Minutes

Common mistakes

  • Treating DevOps as a team name or a tool purchase.
  • Renaming ops to "DevOps team" while keeping the same wall.
  • Automating deployment without automated tests or monitoring.
  • Measuring individuals instead of team outcomes.

How to answer in the interview

"DevOps is a culture and set of practices that brings development and operations together so we can deliver changes fast and reliably. Instead of handing code over a wall, the team that builds a service also runs it, and failures lead to blameless postmortems. The core principles are automating builds, tests, deployments and infrastructure; shipping small changes frequently with fast feedback from CI and monitoring; continuous improvement; and measuring outcomes, typically with the DORA metrics: deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate and time to restore. Good teams improve speed and stability together."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which DORA metric first for a monthly-deploy team? Usually lead time or deployment frequency, by shrinking batch size and automating the pipeline.
  • DevOps vs SRE? SRE is one concrete implementation focused on reliability with SLOs and error budgets.
  • What is platform engineering? Building an internal self-service platform so product teams get DevOps capabilities without reinventing them.
