What should a company generative AI usage policy and governance process cover?

LLMOps & RAG · concept · levels mid, senior, lead

In one line

A good generative AI policy is practical, not just prohibitive: it lists approved tools and models and how to request new ones, sets data classification rules for what may be entered, keeps humans accountable with review for impactful outputs, requires risk assessment and approval of new use cases (privacy, security, bias, EU AI Act, IP), covers transparency, and includes training, monitoring and incident reporting.

Detailed explanation

Banning AI outright doesn't work; people use consumer tools anyway (shadow AI), with no control over data. A policy should make the safe path the easy path.

Approved tools. A list of sanctioned assistants, coding tools and APIs with enterprise agreements (no training on company data, defined retention, SSO), plus a lightweight process for requesting new tools, with security and privacy review.

Data rules by classification. For example: public and internal data allowed in approved tools; confidential data only in approved enterprise tools with DPAs; restricted data (health, payment, HR cases, source code of critical systems, depending on the company) only in specifically approved setups or never. Clear examples help more than abstract rules.

Accountability and human review. The person using an output is responsible for it. Human review is required for customer-facing decisions, code merged to production, legal or financial content and anything published.

Use-case governance. New AI use cases, especially customer-facing or automated decision-making, go through a risk assessment proportional to impact: privacy (DPIA under GDPR where needed), security, bias and fairness, reliability, and regulatory classification under the EU AI Act, which has obligations phasing in from 2025 onwards, with stricter rules for high-risk systems. Intellectual property: licensing of training data and generated content, open-source licence issues in generated code, confidentiality of inputs.

Transparency. Inform customers when they interact with AI or receive AI-generated content where required or appropriate; label synthetic media.

Enablement and oversight. Training on effective and safe use, a register of AI systems in use, usage monitoring via gateways or tool admin consoles, and a clear channel for reporting incidents (data leakage, harmful outputs). Review the policy regularly; the field moves fast.

How it works

  1. Form a cross-functional group: legal, security, privacy, IT, business.
  2. Publish approved tools and data rules with examples.
  3. Define the use-case intake and risk tiers.
  4. Maintain an AI system register.
  5. Train staff; monitor usage; handle incidents.
  6. Review the policy at least annually.

Example

Use case: AI drafts replies to customer complaints
Risk tier: Medium (customer-facing, human sends final reply)
Data: customer name, order history -> confidential -> approved enterprise model, EU region
Controls: human review before sending; PII retention 30 days; tone/accuracy evals
EU AI Act: transparency considerations; not high-risk
Owner: Customer Service lead; review date: 2027-05

Common mistakes

  • Blanket bans that drive shadow AI.
  • Abstract rules without concrete examples.
  • No intake process, so every team improvises.
  • Policy written once and never updated.

How to answer in the interview

"I'd make the policy practical. It lists approved tools and models with proper agreements and a simple way to request new ones, which reduces shadow AI. It sets rules by data classification for what can go into which tools. It makes people accountable for outputs they use, with human review for customer decisions, production code and published content. New use cases get a risk assessment proportional to impact, covering privacy, security, bias, the EU AI Act and IP. It covers transparency to customers, and it's backed by training, usage monitoring, a register of AI systems and an incident reporting process."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Measure compliance? Usage of approved vs unapproved tools from network/CASB logs, intake numbers, audit samples.
  • Who owns it? Cross-functional governance board with a clear executive sponsor.
  • Developers and coding tools? Approved tools, licence filtering, code review still mandatory.
