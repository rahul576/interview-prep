Describe how you handle code review feedback you disagree with.

Teamwork · behavioral · levels entry, mid, senior, lead

In one line

Handle disagreeable review feedback by assuming good intent, asking why to understand the reasoning, responding with data or examples rather than ego, moving to a quick call when threads grow, and accepting or escalating gracefully according to team standards.

Detailed explanation

Code review is one of the most frequent sources of friction in engineering teams, so interviewers use this question to assess collaboration, humility and communication. The ideal candidate sees reviews as a way to improve the code and share knowledge, not as a personal judgement.

Start from good intent: the reviewer is trying to protect the codebase. Before defending your code, ask clarifying questions ("Can you say more about the risk you see here?"). Often the reviewer knows something you do not, such as a past incident, an upcoming change or a performance issue, and you learn something.

If you still disagree, argue with evidence: a benchmark, a test case, a link to the style guide or documentation, or a concrete trade-off. Keep comments about the code, not the person. If a thread goes back and forth more than two or three times, switch to a short call or pair session; text loses tone and long threads become tense. Summarise the outcome back in the PR for transparency.

Distinguish preferences from correctness. For matters of taste, it is usually best to accept the suggestion or follow the team convention, and move on. For significant issues, such as security, architecture or maintainability, it is fine to involve a tech lead or bring it to the team to update the coding standards, so the same debate does not recur. Many teams use labels like "nit:" or "blocking:" to make the weight of each comment clear.

How it works

  1. Read the feedback calmly; assume positive intent.
  2. Ask questions to understand the reasoning.
  3. If you disagree, respond with data, tests or references.
  4. After two or three rounds, move to a call; post a summary.
  5. Accept taste-based suggestions or follow team standards.
  6. For broad issues, propose updating the team standard.

Example

Constructive replies in a pull request:

Reviewer: This loop should use a stream instead.
Me: Good point on readability. I kept the loop because this runs per request
on a hot path; a quick JMH benchmark showed ~15% lower allocation with the
loop (results attached). Happy to switch if you think readability wins here.

Reviewer: nit: rename `tmp` to `pendingOrders`.
Me: Done, thanks.

Common mistakes

  • Taking feedback personally or responding defensively.
  • Long, heated comment threads instead of a quick conversation.
  • Silently ignoring comments or merging anyway.
  • Fighting over pure style preferences.
  • Escalating minor issues to managers.

How to answer in the interview

"I start by assuming good intent and asking why, rather than defending immediately, because often the reviewer has seen something I missed. If I still disagree, I explain my view with evidence, like a benchmark, a test or our style guide, and keep it about the code, not the person. If a thread goes back and forth more than a couple of times, I suggest a quick call, which is faster and keeps the tone friendly, and then I summarise the decision in the PR. For matters of taste I usually accept the suggestion or follow team conventions. If it is a broader issue, we discuss it as a team and update the standard, so we do not repeat the debate."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you give feedback on a junior developer's pull request? Explain the why, separate blocking from optional comments, and praise what is good.
  • What if a reviewer is consistently harsh? Talk to them privately and agree on review norms as a team.
  • How do you keep reviews fast? Small PRs, clear descriptions and agreed review turnaround times.
