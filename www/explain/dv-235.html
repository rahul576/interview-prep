What are RPO and RTO, and how do backup strategies relate to them?

Reliability & DR · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

RPO (recovery point objective) is the maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time; RTO (recovery time objective) is the maximum acceptable downtime; the backup and replication strategy must meet both, follow the 3-2-1 rule with immutable copies, and be proven by regular restore tests.

Detailed explanation

Both values are business decisions, not technical ones: how much does an hour of downtime cost, and what does losing 15 minutes of orders mean in money, regulation and reputation? Engineering then picks the cheapest design that meets them, because lower values cost more.

RPO is driven by how often changes are captured. Nightly backups mean you can lose up to a day. Point-in-time recovery (PITR), which combines base backups with continuous transaction log shipping (PostgreSQL WAL, MySQL binlog, managed options in RDS, Azure SQL or Cloud SQL), gives an RPO of minutes or seconds. Asynchronous replication gives seconds; synchronous replication gives near zero, at the price of write latency.

RTO is driven by how fast you can get running again. Restoring a 2 TB database from object storage and rebuilding servers by hand can take many hours. Infrastructure as code, automation, a warm standby or a promotable replica in another region bring it down to minutes.

The 3-2-1 rule says: keep three copies of data, on two different media or services, with one offsite. Modern practice adds an immutable or air-gapped copy (S3 Object Lock, Azure immutable blob storage, backup vault locks) in a separate account with separate credentials, because ransomware and compromised admin accounts delete backups first. Replication alone is not a backup: it faithfully replicates deletions and corruption.

How it works

  1. Agree RPO and RTO per system with the business (tier 1, 2, 3).
  2. Choose capture method: snapshots, PITR, async or sync replication.
  3. Copy backups to another region and account with immutability.
  4. Automate restore with IaC and scripts.
  5. Run scheduled restore tests and DR drills, measuring achieved RPO/RTO.
  6. Monitor backup jobs and alert on failures or missing backups.

Example

# Restore an RDS PostgreSQL instance to a point in time for a drill
aws rds restore-db-instance-to-point-in-time \
  --source-db-instance-identifier orders-prod \
  --target-db-instance-identifier orders-restore-test \
  --restore-time 2026-11-21T09:45:00Z
# then run checksum / row-count checks and record how long it took (RTO)

Common mistakes

  • Never testing restores.
  • Backups in the same account and region as production.
  • Treating replication or RAID as a backup.
  • Not including configuration, secrets and IaC in the recovery plan.
  • One RPO/RTO for everything instead of tiering.

How to answer in the interview

"RPO is how much data we can afford to lose, measured in time, and RTO is how long the service can be down before it has to be back. They come from the business and drive cost. Nightly backups give an RPO of up to a day, point-in-time recovery gets it to minutes, and synchronous replication to near zero. A short RTO needs automation and a warm standby or promotable replica. I follow 3-2-1, three copies, two media, one offsite, with an immutable copy in a separate account against ransomware. Most importantly, we test restores regularly and measure the real RPO and RTO."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why is an untested backup not a backup? You don't know if it is complete, restorable or fast enough until you restore it.
  • How do you protect against ransomware? Immutable, separate-account backups with separate credentials and MFA.
  • Is replication enough? No, it copies deletions and corruption; you still need point-in-time backups.
