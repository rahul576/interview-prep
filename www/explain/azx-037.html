An entire Azure region becomes unavailable and your application is down. What should your design have prepared, and what do you do now?

Azure · scenario · levels senior, lead

In one line

A regional outage should trigger a rehearsed DR runbook: the design prepared RTO/RPO per service, a secondary region with replicated data and IaC, and global routing (Front Door/Traffic Manager); in the incident you confirm scope, declare, fail over data and compute, verify, communicate, and later fail back carefully.

Detailed explanation

What the design should have prepared:

  • Tiering: RTO and RPO per service agreed with the business. Not everything needs multi-region.
  • Data replication: SQL Database failover groups (with a read-write listener that follows the primary), Cosmos DB multi-region with service-managed failover, GZRS/RA-GZRS storage (customer-initiated failover), geo-replicated ACR, Key Vault (paired-region replication or a vault per region), Service Bus geo-replication or geo-disaster recovery.
  • Compute: IaC (Bicep/Terraform) deploys the secondary environment; for low RTO it runs warm at reduced scale (warm standby) or active-active.
  • Routing: Azure Front Door or Traffic Manager with health probes in front of both regions.
  • Dependencies: identity, DNS, secrets, certificates, CI/CD agents, monitoring and third-party allow-lists that include secondary IPs.
  • Drills: regular failover tests, because untested DR plans almost always fail on a forgotten detail.

During the incident:

  1. Confirm scope with Azure Service Health and your own probes; is it the whole region, one service, or your app?
  2. Declare a disaster according to criteria, so people don't wait hoping for recovery while RTO burns. The decision owner is pre-agreed.
  3. Fail over data: forced failover of SQL failover groups (accepting the RPO), Cosmos DB regional failover, storage account failover if needed.
  4. Scale up compute in the secondary region and let Front Door shift traffic, or switch it.
  5. Verify with smoke tests and dashboards; watch for data gaps.
  6. Communicate via status page and stakeholders at regular intervals.

Afterwards, fail back in a controlled window once the primary is healthy and data is resynchronised, and run a post-incident review.

How it works

  1. Service Health alert and probes fire.
  2. Incident commander follows the DR runbook.
  3. Data failover, compute scale-out, traffic shift.
  4. Smoke tests; status updates.
  5. Stabilise in secondary; reconcile data.
  6. Planned failback and review.

Example

# Forced failover of a SQL failover group to the secondary region
az sql failover-group set-primary -g rg-data-neu -s sql-orders-neu -n fg-orders \
  --allow-data-loss
# Cosmos DB: promote secondary
az cosmosdb failover-priority-change -g rg-data -n cosmos-orders \
  --failover-policies northeurope=0 westeurope=1
# Scale secondary AKS node pool
az aks nodepool scale -g rg-aks-neu --cluster-name aks-neu -n apps --node-count 12

Common mistakes

  • Hidden single-region dependencies (Key Vault, private DNS, CI agents, identity providers).
  • Secondary subscription without enough quota.
  • Waiting hours before declaring.
  • Forgetting to test failback.

How to answer in the interview

"If the design is right, this is a rehearsed procedure. We'd have RTO and RPO per service, data replicated to a second region with SQL failover groups, multi-region Cosmos DB and GZRS storage, infrastructure as code for the secondary, and Front Door routing by health. In the incident I confirm scope with Service Health, declare a disaster per our criteria, fail over the databases accepting the RPO, scale up compute in the secondary and let Front Door shift traffic, then verify with smoke tests while communicating status. Later we fail back in a controlled window. Regular drills are what make this work."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Most forgotten dependencies? Key Vault, DNS, identity, certificates, CI/CD, quotas and partner allow-lists.
  • Microsoft-managed vs customer-initiated failover? Rely on your own initiated failover; don't wait for Microsoft.
  • Availability zones vs regions? Zones handle datacentre failures; regions handle regional outages.
