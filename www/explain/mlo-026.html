How do you choose between SageMaker, Azure Machine Learning and Vertex AI, or a self-built Kubernetes ML platform?

MLOps · design · levels senior, lead

In one line

SageMaker, Azure ML and Vertex AI all cover training, pipelines, registry, endpoints and monitoring; the strongest factor is alignment with the company's main cloud and data platform. A self-built Kubernetes platform (Kubeflow, KServe, MLflow) gives portability and control but needs a strong platform team. Evaluate total cost, GPU access, security integration, compliance and lock-in.

Detailed explanation

The three managed platforms are broadly comparable: managed notebooks, CPU and GPU training jobs, pipelines, experiment tracking, model registry, managed online and batch endpoints, model monitoring and, increasingly, access to foundation models (Bedrock alongside SageMaker, Azure AI Foundry alongside Azure ML, Vertex AI Model Garden and Gemini). Feature-by-feature comparisons rarely decide the matter.

What decides it is alignment. Identity (IAM, Entra ID), networking (VPCs, private endpoints), data (S3 and Redshift, ADLS and Fabric or Databricks, BigQuery), security tooling and procurement already exist in the company's main cloud. Choosing that cloud's ML platform means data doesn't cross clouds, security reviews are familiar, and the team can get a model to production in weeks.

A self-built platform on Kubernetes—Kubeflow Pipelines, KServe, MLflow, Kueue, Argo CD—gives portability across clouds and on-premises, fine-grained control, open standards and sometimes lower cost at large scale or with existing GPU clusters. But it needs a platform team to operate, upgrade, secure and support it, and the people cost is often larger than the cloud bill saved.

Many organisations choose a hybrid: managed platform for most work, open components (MLflow, ONNX, standard containers) to reduce lock-in.

How it works

  1. Identify the primary cloud and data platform.
  2. List requirements: GPU types and quotas, regions, compliance, latency, on-prem needs.
  3. Estimate total cost: compute, licences and platform staff.
  4. Run a pilot with one real use case end to end.
  5. Assess lock-in and exit paths; prefer open formats.
  6. Decide and define golden paths.

Example

Company: Azure-first, data in Databricks on Azure, Entra ID everywhere.
Decision: Azure ML for training and endpoints, MLflow (Databricks) for tracking/registry,
ONNX/containers for portability. Revisit self-hosted KServe if GPU spend > threshold.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a different cloud's ML platform than where the data lives.
  • Underestimating the people cost of a self-built platform.
  • Ignoring GPU quotas and regional availability.
  • No exit plan for proprietary formats.

How to answer in the interview

"All three managed platforms cover notebooks, training, pipelines, registry, endpoints and monitoring, so features rarely decide it. The biggest factor is alignment with the company's main cloud and data platform, because identity, networking, data and security are already there. A self-built Kubernetes platform with Kubeflow, KServe and MLflow gives portability and control, but needs a strong platform team. I'd evaluate total cost including people, GPU availability, security and compliance, and lock-in, and often use open formats like MLflow and ONNX on a managed platform."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which parts are worth building yourself? Thin glue: templates, golden paths, governance integrations; rarely the core serving engine.
  • How do you reduce lock-in? Containers, open model formats, MLflow, IaC.
  • When does self-built pay off? Large GPU fleets, multi-cloud or on-prem requirements and a capable platform team.
