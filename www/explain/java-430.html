How do you design a system for high availability, for example 99.99 percent?

Architecture · concept · levels lead

In one line

99.99% availability allows only about 52 minutes of downtime a year, so you need redundancy across zones (and often regions) with no single point of failure, health checks and automated failover, graceful degradation (timeouts, bulkheads, circuit breakers), safe deployments and regularly tested recovery.

Detailed explanation

Start by translating the number into a budget: 99.9% is about 8.8 hours a year, 99.99% about 52.6 minutes, 99.999% about 5 minutes. At four nines, any incident needing a human to wake up, log in and diagnose consumes most of the year's budget, so recovery must be automated.

Availability also multiplies across serial dependencies: if a request depends on five components each at 99.99%, the composite is roughly 99.95%. Every critical dependency (database, cache, identity provider, DNS, third-party APIs) must be at least as available, or be made optional.

Redundancy: run stateless services across at least three availability zones with capacity to lose one (N+1). Databases use synchronous replication across zones with automatic failover (Aurora, Cloud SQL HA, Patroni for PostgreSQL). Load balancers are managed and multi-zone. For regional outages, consider active-active or warm standby in another region, with data replication and DNS/global load balancer failover; this is expensive, so tie it to business need.

Resilience patterns: timeouts on every call, retries with backoff and jitter (bounded), circuit breakers (Resilience4j), bulkheads isolating thread pools, load shedding and graceful degradation (show cached recommendations instead of failing checkout).

Change is the biggest cause of outages: use canary or blue-green deployments, feature flags, automated rollback on SLO burn, and backward-compatible schema changes. Test recovery: game days, chaos experiments, failover drills, restore-from-backup tests. Define SLOs and error budgets and alert on burn rate.

How it works

  1. Define SLO and error budget per user journey.
  2. Map dependencies; remove or make redundant every SPOF.
  3. Deploy across 3 AZs with autoscaling and N+1 capacity.
  4. Use managed DB with automatic multi-AZ failover; test it.
  5. Add timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads and fallbacks.
  6. Canary deploys with automated rollback.
  7. Run regular game days and track MTTD/MTTR.

Example

resilience4j:
  circuitbreaker:
    instances:
      recommendations:
        slidingWindowSize: 50
        failureRateThreshold: 50
        waitDurationInOpenState: 30s
  timelimiter:
    instances:
      recommendations:
        timeoutDuration: 300ms
  bulkhead:
    instances:
      recommendations:
        maxConcurrentCalls: 20

Common mistakes

  • Promising four nines while depending on a single-AZ database.
  • Ignoring composite availability of dependencies.
  • Untested failover that fails when needed.
  • Unbounded retries causing retry storms.
  • Big-bang deployments without rollback.

How to answer in the interview

"First I translate the target: 99.99% is about 52 minutes of downtime a year, so recovery must be automatic and every critical dependency must be equally available. I'd remove single points of failure with redundancy across three zones, a database with automatic multi-AZ failover, and possibly a second region. Health checks and automated failover replace human reaction. The app degrades gracefully with timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads and fallbacks. Most outages come from changes, so I'd use canaries and fast rollback, and regularly test recovery with game days."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which dependencies usually limit availability? The primary database, DNS, identity provider and third-party APIs.
  • Active-active vs active-passive regions? Active-active gives faster failover but needs conflict handling; passive is simpler but slower.
  • How do you measure it? SLIs from the user's perspective, e.g. successful requests over total, via synthetic and real traffic.
