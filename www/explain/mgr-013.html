How do you say no to a senior stakeholder?

Stakeholder Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Saying no well means understanding the underlying need, explaining trade-offs against current priorities, offering alternatives, escalating to the right forum if needed, and preserving the relationship.

Detailed explanation

Senior stakeholders ask for things because they have goals and pressures. A blunt "no" damages the relationship; a reluctant "yes" overloads the team and puts existing commitments at risk. The skill is to say "no, but" or "yes, if", in a way that makes the trade-off visible and keeps you seen as a partner.

Start with curiosity: what problem are they trying to solve, by when, and why now? Often the request is a proposed solution, and a simpler alternative can meet the actual need. Listening first also earns credibility for your answer.

Next, make the trade-off concrete. Teams have finite capacity, so saying yes to this means saying no to something else. Show the current priorities, ideally agreed with leadership, and explain what would slip: "If we do this now, the security audit fixes move from June to August." This shifts the discussion from "engineering is blocking me" to a prioritisation decision.

Offer alternatives: a smaller version, a later date, a manual workaround, or another team better placed to help. If you still disagree, do not fight it out privately; suggest taking it to the right forum, such as the product leadership or portfolio review, where priorities can be decided openly. Keep the tone respectful, confirm the outcome in writing, and follow up later, for example when the request can be scheduled.

How it works

  1. Listen and ask about the underlying goal and urgency.
  2. Check the request against agreed priorities and capacity.
  3. Explain the trade-off with concrete impact.
  4. Offer alternatives: smaller scope, later date, other team.
  5. Escalate openly to the decision forum if disagreement remains.
  6. Confirm in writing and follow up.

Example

A short written follow-up after a conversation:

Hi Anita, thanks for the discussion today. To confirm: the team is fully
committed to the PCI audit fixes until 30 June. Building the custom partner
report now would push those fixes to August, which risks the audit.
Options: (1) we deliver a CSV export by Friday as a stop-gap, (2) we schedule
the full report for July. If you would like to re-prioritise against the audit,
I suggest we bring it to Thursday's product council. Happy to discuss.

Common mistakes

  • Saying no before understanding the need.
  • Saying yes to avoid conflict, then missing commitments.
  • Making it personal or emotional.
  • No alternative offered.
  • Escalating behind the stakeholder's back.

How to answer in the interview

"I start by understanding their need and the goal behind the request, because sometimes there is a simpler way to meet it. Then I explain the trade-offs against our current priorities very concretely: if we do this now, what slips and what it costs. I offer alternatives, like a smaller version, a later date, or another team better placed to help. If we still disagree, I suggest taking it to the right leadership forum so priorities are decided openly, not fought out between us. I keep it respectful, confirm the outcome in writing, and follow up, so the relationship stays strong."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Can you tell me about a time saying no was the right call? Give a STAR story where protecting a priority avoided a bigger problem.
  • What if the stakeholder is your manager's manager? Same approach, but involve your manager early.
  • How do you avoid being seen as negative? Lead with what you can do and with data.
