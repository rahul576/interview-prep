What is the difference between high availability and fault tolerance in AWS?

Cloud (AWS) · concept · levels entry

In one line

High availability means minimal downtime with quick recovery when something fails (for example an RDS Multi-AZ failover of a minute or two); fault tolerance means no interruption at all, because redundant components are already serving; both rely on redundancy across AZs and no single point of failure, and stronger guarantees cost more.

Detailed explanation

Availability is the percentage of time a system is usable: 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours of downtime per year, 99.99% about 53 minutes. A highly available system is designed so failures cause only short disruptions. It detects the failure and recovers automatically: a standby takes over, an instance is replaced, DNS fails over. Users may see errors or a pause during the switch. RDS Multi-AZ with one standby is the classic example: the primary fails, RDS promotes the standby and flips the DNS endpoint, typically within one to two minutes, during which connections drop.

A fault-tolerant system keeps working without any noticeable interruption when a component fails, because the capacity that handles the load is already active in redundant form. An application running on instances in three AZs behind an ALB, with enough capacity that losing one AZ leaves the other two able to serve all traffic, is fault tolerant to an AZ failure: the load balancer stops routing to the failed zone and users notice nothing. S3 and DynamoDB, which replicate synchronously across AZs, behave similarly. Aurora, with storage across three AZs, is fault tolerant for storage while compute failover is still HA.

Both rely on eliminating single points of failure: multiple AZs, load balancers, Auto Scaling with health checks, replicated data, and static stability (enough pre-provisioned capacity so you do not depend on launching new resources during an outage). Fault tolerance needs active redundancy and over-provisioning, so it costs more. Disaster recovery is a further layer, about recovering from region-level events, measured with RTO and RPO. You choose the level according to business impact.

How it works

  1. Identify single points of failure.
  2. Add redundancy across AZs.
  3. HA: standby plus automatic failover.
  4. Fault tolerance: active-active with spare capacity.
  5. Health checks detect and route around failures.
  6. Test with game days and fault injection (AWS FIS).

Example

Component      HA design                          Fault-tolerant design
Web tier       ASG min 2, replaces failed node    ASG across 3 AZs, N+1 capacity, ALB
Database       RDS Multi-AZ (1-2 min failover)    Aurora / DynamoDB multi-AZ storage,
                                                  app retries absorb brief failover
Cache          Single node, rebuilt on failure    Replication group, multi-AZ

Common mistakes

  • Treating the terms as synonyms.
  • Running multi-AZ but with too little capacity to survive one AZ loss.
  • Forgetting single points such as one NAT gateway.
  • Never testing failover.

How to answer in the interview

"High availability means the system stays usable most of the time and recovers quickly from failures, with maybe a short disruption, like an RDS Multi-AZ failover that takes a minute or two. Fault tolerance is stronger: the system keeps working with no interruption because redundant components are already serving, for example an app across three AZs behind a load balancer with enough capacity that losing a zone is invisible. Both come from redundancy and removing single points of failure. Fault tolerance needs active spare capacity, so it costs more, and I'd pick the level based on business requirements."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Is RDS Multi-AZ fault tolerant or HA? Highly available: failover causes a brief interruption.
  • What is static stability? Having enough capacity already running so recovery doesn't depend on launching resources.
  • RTO vs RPO? RTO is how long recovery takes; RPO is how much data you can afford to lose.
