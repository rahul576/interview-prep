What are microservices?

Microservices · concept · levels entry

In one line

Microservices is an architectural style where an application is built from small, independently deployable services organised around business capabilities, each owning its data and communicating over APIs or messaging, which trades simplicity for autonomy and scalability.

Detailed explanation

In a traditional monolith, all features, orders, payments, catalogue, users, live in one codebase and are deployed together as one unit. That is simple at first, but as the system and the number of teams grow, every change requires building, testing and releasing everything, and teams step on each other.

With microservices, the application is split into services such as order, payment, inventory and notification. Each service:

  • focuses on one business capability (a bounded context in domain-driven design terms), not a technical layer;
  • runs in its own process, usually a container, and is deployed independently through its own pipeline;
  • owns its data, with its own database or schema that other services do not access directly;
  • communicates through well-defined interfaces: synchronous REST or gRPC calls, or asynchronous events and messages over Kafka or RabbitMQ;
  • is owned end to end by one small team, which can choose fitting technology and release on its own schedule.

The benefits are independent releases, independent scaling (run more instances of the busy catalogue service), fault isolation and team autonomy. The price is that you now operate a distributed system: network calls can fail or be slow, data consistency across services needs sagas and events, and you need strong DevOps practices: CI/CD, containers and orchestration (Kubernetes), centralised logging, metrics and distributed tracing, service discovery and an API gateway.

That is why microservices pay off mainly for large systems and organisations with several teams. For a small team or a new product, a well-structured modular monolith is usually the better start.

How it works

  1. Identify business capabilities (orders, payments, inventory).
  2. Build each as a separate service with its own database.
  3. Package each as a container with its own CI/CD pipeline.
  4. Expose APIs; publish events for important state changes.
  5. Run them on a platform like Kubernetes, behind an API gateway.
  6. Observe with logs, metrics and traces.

Example

Client ──> API Gateway ──> order-service ──(REST)──> inventory-service
                              │                         [inventory DB]
                         [orders DB]
                              │ publishes OrderPlaced (Kafka)
                              ├──> payment-service      [payments DB]
                              └──> notification-service (sends email)

A spike in browsing during a sale is handled by scaling only the catalogue and inventory services, while the payment team deploys a fix without touching orders.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking "micro" means as small as possible; boundaries matter more than size.
  • Splitting by technical layers (UI service, DB service).
  • Sharing one database between services.
  • Adopting microservices without automation and monitoring.

How to answer in the interview

"Microservices is an architectural style where an application is built as a set of small services, each focused on one business capability such as orders, payments or inventory. Each runs in its own process, is deployed independently and owns its own database. They communicate through APIs like REST or gRPC, or asynchronously through messages on Kafka. A small team can own a service end to end and release it independently. The trade-off is distributed-system complexity: network failures, data consistency, observability and operations, so it pays off mainly for larger systems and organisations."

Likely follow-up questions

  • When would you not recommend microservices? Small teams, early products with unclear domains, or no DevOps maturity.
  • How small should a service be? Sized by business capability and team ownership, not lines of code.
  • What is a bounded context? A DDD boundary within which a model and its terms have one consistent meaning.
