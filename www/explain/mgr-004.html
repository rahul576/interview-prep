How do you balance technical debt against new feature work?

Leadership & Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Make technical debt visible and quantified in business terms (incidents, delivery speed, cost), agree dedicated capacity (often 15-20%), apply the boy scout rule alongside features, plan large items as projects with outcomes, and prioritise jointly with product.

Detailed explanation

Debt is not always bad; taking it on deliberately to hit a market window can be smart. The problem is unmanaged debt that silently slows every change and causes incidents. The interviewer wants to see that you can translate engineering concerns into business language and create a sustainable system rather than periodic crises.

Visibility: keep debt items in the same backlog as features, each with a short description of impact: "every change to pricing takes 3 extra days because of duplicated rules", "this library is end-of-life and has a known CVE", "this flaky test suite costs 5 developer-hours a week". Use data: incident counts, cycle time in affected areas, cloud cost, onboarding time, security findings.

Business framing: product managers prioritise impact, not code quality. "Refactor the order module" loses; "cut lead time for checkout changes from two weeks to one and remove the cause of four incidents last quarter" wins.

Capacity: agree a standing allocation, commonly 15-20% of each sprint for debt, upgrades and platform work, so it does not need to be argued for every time. Combine with the boy scout rule: when touching code for a feature, leave it a bit better, included in the feature's estimate.

Large items (framework migration, database replacement) are planned as projects with milestones and measurable outcomes, competing on the roadmap transparently.

Joint prioritisation with product makes it a shared decision; review the debt register quarterly, and track whether investments paid off.

How it works

  1. Collect debt items with impact and evidence.
  2. Score by impact vs effort; tag risk (security, reliability, speed).
  3. Agree a capacity percentage with product.
  4. Bundle small fixes into features (boy scout rule).
  5. Plan big items as roadmap projects with outcomes.
  6. Review quarterly; report results.

Example

Situation: Our checkout team spent most of each sprint on features, and incidents in checkout had doubled in two quarters, while the product manager saw refactoring requests as "engineers wanting nice code". Task: As engineering manager, I needed a sustainable balance. Action: I gathered data: six of nine checkout incidents traced to the legacy promotion engine, and changes there took about twice as long as elsewhere. I presented this as a cost to the PM, roughly 25% of the team's capacity lost to incidents and slow changes. We agreed 20% capacity per sprint for debt, and a three-sprint project to replace the promotion engine, with a goal of halving incidents and cycle time in that area. Engineers applied the boy scout rule to the surrounding code during feature work. Result: Over the next two quarters checkout incidents dropped by about 60%, promotion changes went from about eight days to four, and the PM began proposing debt items herself.

Common mistakes

  • Arguing for debt work in purely technical terms.
  • A separate "tech debt backlog" nobody prioritises.
  • Big-bang "refactoring sprints" once a year.
  • Hiding refactoring inside inflated feature estimates.
  • Never measuring whether the work paid off.

How to answer in the interview

"I make technical debt visible in the same backlog as features and describe each item by its business impact: incidents, how much it slows delivery, cost or security risk, because product prioritises impact rather than code quality. We agree a standing capacity, typically 15 to 20% of each sprint, and apply a boy scout rule to improve code alongside features. Large items like a framework upgrade become projects with clear outcomes. Prioritisation happens jointly with product, so it's a shared decision rather than engineers asking for permission, and we track whether it paid off."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Convincing leadership to fund six months of refactoring? Business case with data, incremental milestones delivering value, and the cost of doing nothing.
  • Product refuses any debt time? Show the trend in incidents and lead time; start with a small experiment and measure.
  • How do you prevent new debt? Definition of done, reviews, ADRs for deliberate shortcuts with a payback plan.
