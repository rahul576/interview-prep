Explain the AWS disaster recovery strategies and how you choose between them.

Cloud (AWS) · design · levels senior

In one line

AWS describes four DR strategies along a cost and recovery curve: backup and restore (hours), pilot light (tens of minutes), warm standby (minutes) and multi-site active-active (near zero), chosen from the business RTO and RPO and budget, and proven by regular testing.

Detailed explanation

RTO (recovery time objective) is how long the service may be down; RPO (recovery point objective) is how much data may be lost. They are business decisions per workload, and they drive the architecture.

Backup and restore: data is backed up and copied to another region (AWS Backup cross-region copies, S3 replication), and everything else is rebuilt from infrastructure as code and AMIs after a disaster. It is cheapest, but RTO is hours and RPO equals the backup interval, unless continuous backups shrink it.

Pilot light: the core data layer is live in the recovery region, such as an Aurora Global Database secondary, DynamoDB global tables or cross-region read replicas, while application servers are defined but scaled to zero or stopped. Recovery means promoting the database, scaling up compute and switching DNS: tens of minutes, with RPO of seconds.

Warm standby: a scaled-down but fully functional copy of the whole stack runs continuously and can even take some traffic. Failover means scaling it up and redirecting traffic: RTO of minutes.

Multi-site active-active: both regions serve production traffic with data replicated; losing one means routing everything to the other. RTO and RPO are near zero, but cost roughly doubles and data consistency is complex.

Choose by tiering workloads: a reporting tool may accept backup and restore, a checkout must be warm standby or active-active. AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery offers block-level replication for servers, and Route 53 Application Recovery Controller provides readiness checks and safe routing controls. Whatever the choice, run game days, keep runbooks current, and verify quotas and capacity in the DR region.

How it works

  1. Agree RTO and RPO per workload with the business.
  2. Map each to a strategy and estimate cost.
  3. Replicate data: backups, replicas, global tables.
  4. Keep infrastructure deployable from IaC in the DR region.
  5. Automate failover: promote data, scale compute, switch traffic.
  6. Test regularly and measure actual RTO and RPO.

Example

Pilot light failover runbook steps as commands:

# 1. Promote Aurora Global Database secondary
aws rds failover-global-cluster --global-cluster-identifier shop-global \
  --target-db-cluster-identifier arn:aws:rds:eu-central-1:111122223333:cluster:shop-dr \
  --allow-data-loss
# 2. Scale the application tier in the DR region
aws ecs update-service --cluster shop-dr --service web --desired-count 12
# 3. Flip traffic with an ARC routing control or Route 53 failover
aws route53-recovery-cluster update-routing-control-state \
  --routing-control-arn $DR_CONTROL --routing-control-state On

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a strategy without agreed RTO/RPO numbers.
  • Never testing, then discovering missing quotas, AMIs or secrets in the DR region.
  • Forgetting DNS TTLs, certificates and third-party allow lists.
  • Replicating data but not configuration, secrets or container images.

How to answer in the interview

"AWS defines four strategies. Backup and restore copies backups to another region and rebuilds from IaC: cheapest, but hours of RTO and data loss up to the backup interval. Pilot light keeps the data layer live in the DR region, like an Aurora Global Database secondary, with compute scaled to zero, so recovery takes tens of minutes. Warm standby runs a scaled-down full copy, giving minutes of RTO. Active-active serves from both regions with near-zero RTO and RPO at the highest cost and complexity. I pick per workload from the business RTO, RPO and budget, and test failover regularly."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What usually breaks during a first real DR test? Missing quotas, hard-coded region values, secrets, DNS TTLs and stale runbooks.
  • How measure RPO? Replication lag metrics and backup timestamps during tests.
  • Where does Elastic Disaster Recovery fit? Server-level continuous replication for lift-and-shift workloads, near pilot light.
