How do you handle highly imbalanced classes, such as fraud at 0.1 percent, in training and evaluation?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

With heavy imbalance, accuracy is misleading; evaluate with precision, recall and PR-AUC plus business metrics, train with class weights or resampling (then recalibrate), ensure enough positives in splits (stratified or time-based), tune the threshold to operational capacity, and monitor alert volumes and precision in production.

Detailed explanation

At 0.1 percent fraud, a model that always says "not fraud" is 99.9 percent accurate and catches nothing. ROC-AUC can also look flattering, because the huge number of true negatives makes the false positive rate tiny even when most alerts are wrong.

Evaluation. Use precision (share of alerts that are fraud), recall (share of fraud caught), the precision-recall curve and PR-AUC, and metrics at the operating point, such as recall at a fixed alert budget or precision at 1 percent of transactions reviewed. Add business metrics: fraud value caught (not just count), false declines and alert volume.

Training. Options: class weights (for example scale_pos_weight in XGBoost, class_weight in scikit-learn) make errors on the rare class cost more; undersampling the majority class speeds training and focuses learning; oversampling or SMOTE duplicates or synthesises positives, with risk of overfitting. Resampling and weighting distort predicted probabilities, so recalibrate (Platt or isotonic on unresampled validation data) if probabilities matter. Often a strong gradient boosting model with modest weighting plus threshold tuning beats elaborate resampling. Focal loss helps in deep learning.

Splits. Ensure validation and test sets contain enough positives for stable metrics: stratified splits, or time-based splits covering long enough periods. Resample only the training set, never validation or test.

Threshold. Choose the operating point by investigator capacity and costs, not 0.5.

Production. Monitor alert volumes, precision of reviewed alerts and caught fraud value. Because positives are rare, small drifts translate into big workload changes, which is a useful early signal.

How it works

  1. Establish metrics: PR-AUC, recall at alert budget, value caught.
  2. Split by time or stratify, keeping enough positives.
  3. Train with class weights or training-set-only resampling.
  4. Calibrate on untouched validation data.
  5. Select the threshold by capacity and cost.
  6. Monitor alert volume and precision in production.

Example

from xgboost import XGBClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import average_precision_score, precision_recall_curve

ratio = (y_train == 0).sum() / (y_train == 1).sum()      # ~999
model = XGBClassifier(n_estimators=600, max_depth=6, scale_pos_weight=ratio ** 0.5,
                      eval_metric="aucpr")
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
p = model.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1]
print("PR-AUC", average_precision_score(y_val, p))

k = int(0.005 * len(p))                                   # review top 0.5 %
top = p.argsort()[::-1][:k]
print("recall@0.5%", y_val.iloc[top].sum() / y_val.sum())

Common mistakes

  • Reporting accuracy or only ROC-AUC.
  • Resampling before the split, leaking duplicates into test.
  • Using resampled-model probabilities without recalibration.
  • Too few positives in the test set for stable metrics.

How to answer in the interview

"At 0.1 percent fraud, predicting 'no fraud' is 99.9 percent accurate and useless, so I evaluate with precision, recall and PR-AUC, plus fraud value caught and alert volume. In training I use class weights or resampling on the training set only, and recalibrate probabilities afterwards because resampling distorts them. Splits need enough positives, stratified or time-based. The threshold is tuned to what investigators can handle. In production I monitor alert volumes and precision of reviewed alerts, since small drifts show up quickly as workload changes."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why might oversampling look better offline than it is? Duplicated positives leak into validation if resampled before splitting, and overfitting to repeats.
  • Why not ROC-AUC? True negatives dominate, hiding poor precision.
  • Anomaly detection instead? Useful when labels are very scarce, often combined with supervised models.
