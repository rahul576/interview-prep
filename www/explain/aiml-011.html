How does k-means clustering work, and how do you choose the number of clusters?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

K-means alternates between assigning points to the nearest centroid and moving centroids to the mean of their points until stable; choose k with the elbow method, silhouette score and, crucially, whether the segments are useful to the business.

Detailed explanation

K-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares (inertia): the total squared distance from each point to its cluster centre. It starts with k initial centroids, ideally using k-means++, which spreads initial centres apart and gives better, more consistent results than random starts. Because it can converge to a local optimum, it is run several times (n_init) and the best run is kept.

Since it uses Euclidean distance, features must be numeric and on comparable scales; otherwise income in rupees dominates age in years. It implicitly assumes clusters that are roughly spherical, similar in size and density, and it assigns every point to some cluster, so outliers pull centroids around. For irregular shapes or noise, DBSCAN or HDBSCAN (density-based, finds outliers, no k needed), Gaussian mixture models (soft, elliptical clusters) or hierarchical clustering may fit better.

Choosing k has no single correct answer. The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the point where adding clusters stops giving big gains. The silhouette score (from -1 to 1) compares how close each point is to its own cluster versus the nearest other cluster; higher is better. Other options are the Davies-Bouldin index or the gap statistic. In practice, stability (do clusters persist across samples?) and actionability (can marketing design a campaign for each segment?) matter as much as the numbers.

How it works

  1. Scale features and pick a range of k values.
  2. Initialise k centroids with k-means++.
  3. Assign each point to its nearest centroid.
  4. Recompute each centroid as the mean of its points.
  5. Repeat steps 3-4 until assignments stop changing; compare k using inertia and silhouette.

Example

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(customers[["recency", "frequency", "monetary"]])
for k in range(2, 9):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X)
    print(k, round(km.inertia_), round(silhouette_score(X, km.labels_), 3))

Common mistakes

  • Clustering unscaled features.
  • One-hot encoding many categoricals and running k-means on them as if distances were meaningful.
  • Picking k purely from the elbow plot without checking whether segments make sense.
  • Assuming k-means finds non-spherical shapes or handles outliers well.

How to answer in the interview

"K-means picks k centroids, usually with k-means++, assigns every point to the nearest one, moves each centroid to the mean of its points, and repeats until nothing changes. It needs scaled numeric features, it's sensitive to initialisation so I run it several times, and it assumes roughly round, similar-sized clusters. To pick k I look at the elbow in inertia and the silhouette score, then I check stability and whether the business can actually act on the segments. For odd shapes or noisy data I'd try DBSCAN or a Gaussian mixture."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How would you cluster numeric and categorical data together? K-prototypes, Gower distance with hierarchical clustering, or embeddings.
  • How does DBSCAN differ? Groups dense regions, labels outliers, and doesn't need k.
  • How do you scale k-means to huge data? Mini-batch k-means.
