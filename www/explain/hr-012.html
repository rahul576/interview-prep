How do you explain a complex technical concept to a non-technical stakeholder?

Communication · behavioral · levels entry, mid, senior, lead

In one line

Start from the audience and the decision they need to make, use plain language and analogies, translate into impact on cost, risk and time, support with simple visuals, and check understanding by asking them to play back the decision.

Detailed explanation

Engineers often explain how something works when the stakeholder needs to know what it means for them. A product head deciding whether to fund a database migration does not need replication internals; they need the risk of not doing it, the cost, the timeline and the options.

The interviewer is checking empathy, clarity and influence. Good communicators first ask: who is this person, what do they already know, what decision or action do I need from them? Then they lead with the conclusion (the "bottom line up front"), then add only as much detail as needed.

Analogies bridge the gap: caching is "keeping frequently used files on your desk instead of in the basement archive"; technical debt is "a loan that charges interest every time we change this code"; a load balancer is "a receptionist sending visitors to free counters". Use them, but say where the analogy breaks if that matters to the decision.

Impact framing turns technical facts into business terms: "the outage risk is roughly one major incident per quarter, each costing about X in lost orders". Visuals such as one simple diagram or a before/after chart beat paragraphs. Finally, check understanding actively: rather than "does that make sense?", ask "how would you explain this choice to your team?" or confirm the decision in a short follow-up note.

How it works

  1. Identify the audience and the decision or action needed.
  2. Lead with the conclusion and recommendation.
  3. Explain with plain words and one good analogy.
  4. Quantify impact: cost, risk, time, customer effect.
  5. Show options with trade-offs, ideally in one visual.
  6. Check understanding and follow up in writing.

Example

Situation: Our head of sales wanted to know why a "simple" feature, showing real-time stock across 40 stores, would take six weeks. Task: Explain the complexity and get a decision on scope. Action: I started with the bottom line: "We can show stock that is up to 15 minutes old in one week, or real-time stock in six weeks." I used an analogy: today each store sends a register summary at night like a daily postal letter; real time is like installing a phone line in every store. I drew a single diagram of stores, the central system and the website, and gave costs and risks for each option. I asked her which matters more to customers: precision or getting it before the festive season. Result: She chose the 15-minute version for the season and real time in the next quarter. Afterwards she reused my analogy when explaining the plan to her team.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with technical detail and burying the decision.
  • Jargon and acronyms without explanation.
  • Talking down to the stakeholder.
  • Analogies that are misleading for the actual decision.
  • Asking "does that make sense?" and taking "yes" as proof.

How to answer in the interview

"I start by understanding who I'm talking to and what they need to decide, because usually they need impact and options rather than internals. I lead with the conclusion, use plain language and an analogy, for example explaining caching as keeping frequently used files on your desk instead of in the basement archive. I translate everything into cost, risk and time, and use a simple diagram if it helps. Then I check understanding by asking them to summarise the choice, and I confirm the decision in a short follow-up note."

Likely follow-up questions

  • An analogy you've used? Technical debt as a loan with interest paid on every change.
  • What if they still disagree? Clarify their concern, bring data, offer a small experiment.
  • Written or verbal? Verbal for discussion, a short written summary for the decision record.
