What happens during backlog refinement?

Agile & Scrum · concept · levels entry

In one line

Backlog refinement is an ongoing activity where the product owner and team review upcoming items to clarify requirements and acceptance criteria, split large stories, identify dependencies and risks, estimate, and order the backlog so the top items meet the definition of ready.

Detailed explanation

Without refinement, sprint planning turns into a long requirements workshop, and the team commits to work it does not understand. Refinement, formerly called grooming, spreads that thinking across the sprint. The Scrum Guide describes it as an ongoing activity rather than a fixed event; most teams hold one or two sessions a week, often an hour, and the team typically spends up to about 10 percent of its time on it.

The focus is the next one or two sprints of work. The product owner explains each item: who the user is, what they need and why. The team asks questions, sharpens acceptance criteria, often in Given/When/Then form, and discusses a technical approach at a high level. They spot dependencies on other teams or vendors and risks, and add spikes for unknowns.

Large stories are split into vertical slices: each slice delivers end-to-end value through UI, API and database, rather than horizontal "backend first, frontend later" pieces. Splitting patterns include by workflow step, by business rule, by data variation, or happy path first and edge cases later. The team then estimates, often with planning poker, where simultaneous reveals avoid anchoring.

The outcome is that the top items meet the team's definition of ready: understood, small enough for a sprint, testable, estimated, with dependencies resolved or known. Then sprint planning is quick and commitment is realistic.

How it works

  1. Product owner prepares and orders candidate items before the session.
  2. For each item, discuss the user need and the "why".
  3. Write or refine acceptance criteria; agree how it will be tested.
  4. Identify dependencies, risks and unknowns; create spikes where needed.
  5. Split large stories into vertical slices.
  6. Estimate with planning poker and update the backlog order.

Example

A story before and after refinement:

Before: "As a user I want to manage my addresses"   (too big, unclear)

After split:
PROF-21 As a customer I can add a delivery address        3 pts
  Given I am logged in
  When I submit a valid UK address with postcode
  Then it is saved and shown in my address list
  And invalid postcodes show an inline error
PROF-22 As a customer I can set a default address          2 pts
PROF-23 Spike: postcode lookup vendor API limits (1 day)   time-boxed
Dependency: Checkout team must read default address (link PROF-22 -> CHK-88)

Common mistakes

  • Refining items months ahead that will change anyway.
  • Only the product owner and one tech lead attend, so the team lacks shared understanding.
  • Horizontal splitting that produces stories with no user value on their own.
  • Estimating without clear acceptance criteria.
  • Treating the definition of ready as a rigid gate that blocks all collaboration.

How to answer in the interview

"Backlog refinement is an ongoing session, for us once a week, where the product owner and the team look at the next one or two sprints of backlog items. We clarify what the user needs and why, write or sharpen acceptance criteria, discuss the technical approach and risks, and spot dependencies on other teams. If a story is too big we split it into vertical slices, and for unknowns we add a time-boxed spike. Then we estimate with planning poker. The goal is that the items at the top meet our definition of ready, so sprint planning is quick and we can commit with confidence."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What does "ready" mean for a story? Clear value, testable acceptance criteria, estimated, small enough for a sprint, dependencies known.
  • Who attends refinement? Product owner and developers; sometimes UX, QA or architects as needed.
  • Why planning poker? Simultaneous reveals avoid anchoring and surface different assumptions.
