What is quarterly planning?

Agile & Scrum · concept · levels mid

In one line

Quarterly planning is a longer-horizon session every three months where product and engineering align work with company objectives (often OKRs), agree which epics and initiatives to tackle, check them against team capacity, surface cross-team dependencies and risks, and produce committed and stretch objectives; in SAFe it is called PI planning.

Detailed explanation

Sprints are too short to coordinate large initiatives across several teams, and annual plans are too rigid. A quarter is a useful middle: long enough to deliver meaningful outcomes, short enough to adapt.

Preparation matters. Leadership shares strategy and objectives, often as OKRs (objectives with measurable key results). Product managers bring a prioritised list of initiatives and epics. Engineering brings technical needs: platform upgrades, tech debt, reliability work. Each epic gets a rough size, often with t-shirt sizes or a range of sprints, because detailed estimates three months ahead would be false precision.

During planning, teams compare demand with capacity: the number of people, minus holidays, on-call and support load, and a buffer for unplanned work. Because teams depend on each other, a key output is a dependency map: team A needs an API from team B by sprint 3. Risks are captured and handled, for example with the SAFe ROAM approach (resolved, owned, accepted, mitigated).

In SAFe, this is PI (Program Increment) planning, a one- or two-day event where all teams of an Agile Release Train plan together, typically for 8–12 weeks, producing team PI objectives with business value scores, a program board of features and dependencies, and a confidence vote. Outside SAFe the event is lighter, but the outputs are similar: a roadmap with commitments and stretch goals, broken down into sprints later. The plan is a forecast, revisited monthly or when priorities change.

How it works

  1. Leadership shares objectives and OKRs; product prepares a ranked list of initiatives.
  2. Teams size epics roughly and add technical work.
  3. Capacity is calculated after holidays, on-call, support and buffer.
  4. Teams draft a plan, identify dependencies and risks, and negotiate across teams.
  5. Agree committed objectives and stretch goals; record dependencies on a shared board.
  6. Execute in sprints and review progress against the plan regularly.

Common mistakes

  • Filling capacity to 100 percent with no buffer for incidents and support.
  • Treating the plan as a fixed contract rather than a forecast.
  • Planning teams in isolation and discovering dependencies mid-quarter.
  • Only listing features, without linking them to measurable objectives.

How to answer in the interview

"Quarterly planning is where we look three months ahead. Leadership shares the objectives, often as OKRs, and product brings the initiatives. We break them into epics, size them roughly with t-shirt sizes, and compare that with real capacity after holidays, on-call and support work. A big part is identifying cross-team dependencies and risks early. In SAFe that's called PI planning, with all teams of a release train planning together and agreeing PI objectives. The result is a roadmap with committed objectives and some stretch goals, which we then break down into sprints and revisit when priorities change."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you estimate a quarter when details are unclear? T-shirt sizes mapped to sprint ranges, reference epics, spikes for unknowns and a buffer.
  • What happens if a priority changes mid-quarter? Re-plan openly, showing what drops out to make room.
  • What are stretch goals? Objectives planned but not committed, often 10–20 percent of capacity.
