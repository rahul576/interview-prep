How do you choose evaluation metrics and decision thresholds that match the business problem?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Start from the business decision and the cost of each error type: choose precision, recall, F1 or a cost-weighted metric accordingly (rarely accuracy with imbalance), pick the decision threshold separately on validation data against costs and operational capacity, ensure calibration when probabilities are used directly, and monitor the business outcome.

Detailed explanation

A model metric is a proxy for a business goal, so translate the goal first. In fraud detection, a false negative loses the transaction amount and maybe a chargeback fee, while a false positive declines a good customer, risks a lost sale and a complaint. In medical screening, a missed case is far worse than an extra test, so recall dominates. In marketing, contacting a non-responder costs a little; missing a responder loses margin.

These costs determine the metric. Precision matters when acting is expensive or annoying; recall when missing is expensive; F1 balances both when costs are similar; a cost-weighted metric or expected value is best when costs are known. Accuracy is misleading with imbalanced classes. For ranking tasks, use precision@k, recall@k or NDCG; for threshold-free comparison, ROC-AUC or, under heavy imbalance, PR-AUC.

Most classifiers output scores, so the threshold is a separate decision. Choose it on validation data, not the test set, by plotting precision and recall or expected cost against thresholds, and pick the operating point that matches business constraints—for example, the number of alerts investigators can review per day, or a maximum acceptable false-decline rate.

If probabilities feed directly into pricing, expected-value calculations or ranking across models, they must be calibrated (Platt scaling, isotonic regression), checked with reliability curves or Brier score.

In production, monitor the business outcome—fraud loss, review workload, conversion—not just the model metric, and revisit thresholds when costs or capacity change.

How it works

  1. Write down the decision, actions and cost of FP and FN.
  2. Choose the evaluation metric that reflects those costs.
  3. Train and compare models on that metric.
  4. Sweep thresholds on validation data; compute expected cost and alert volume.
  5. Pick the operating point; confirm on the test set.
  6. Monitor business KPIs and adjust thresholds as conditions change.

Common mistakes

  • Reporting accuracy on a 0.1 percent positive class.
  • Using the default 0.5 threshold.
  • Tuning the threshold on the test set.
  • Treating uncalibrated scores as probabilities.

How to answer in the interview

"I start from the business decision and the cost of each error. In fraud, a missed fraud loses money while a false positive annoys a good customer, so I'd weigh precision and recall or use a cost-weighted metric; accuracy is useless with imbalance. The threshold is a separate decision: on validation data I plot precision, recall or expected cost against thresholds and pick the point that fits business capacity, like how many alerts investigators can handle per day. If probabilities are used directly, the model must be calibrated. In production I watch the business outcome."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How would the threshold change if the fraud team doubled? Lower it to raise recall, since capacity for more alerts exists.
  • ROC-AUC or PR-AUC for rare classes? PR-AUC, since ROC-AUC can look good despite poor precision.
  • How do you check calibration? Reliability diagrams, Brier score and expected calibration error.
