What should you log and audit for MCP tool calls in a regulated environment?

MCP Servers · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

For each tool call record who (user, client application, agent or session, on whose behalf), what (server, tool and version, arguments, outcome, records read or changed), when, and approval decisions, with redaction of sensitive fields, tamper-resistant retention and a link to the conversation trace.

Detailed explanation

In a regulated environment (banking, healthcare, insurance, public sector) you must be able to answer, months later, "who accessed this record, why, and what changed?" An AI agent adds a layer of indirection: the user asked for something, the model decided to call tools, and the tools touched data. The audit trail must capture all three.

Who. The authenticated user, the client application (which host), the agent or session ID, and for delegated or automated runs, on whose behalf the agent acted. For machine identities, the workload or pipeline run.

What. The server, tool name and version (definitions change), the arguments, the outcome or error, and identifiers of the records read or changed. In many regulations access itself must be justified, so reading a patient or customer record is an auditable event, not just modifying it.

When and with what approval. Timestamps and any human approval: who approved, what they saw, and whether they edited the arguments.

Privacy balance. Arguments and results can contain personal data. Store record identifiers rather than full content, and redact or tokenise sensitive fields. Keep detailed payloads, where required, in a separately protected store with stricter access.

Integrity and retention. Logs go to a central, tamper-resistant store: append-only or immutable (WORM) storage, or a SIEM, with retention periods matching regulation, and access to the logs themselves is audited.

Traceability. Each record carries the trace and conversation ID, so investigators can reconstruct why the agent made the call: what the user asked and what content preceded it.

How it works

  1. Gateway or server middleware emits an audit event per tool call.
  2. Event includes identity, tool, redacted arguments, record IDs, outcome, approval.
  3. Events go to an immutable store and the SIEM.
  4. Retention and legal hold policies apply per data class.
  5. Investigators join audit events with conversation traces by trace ID.

Common mistakes

  • Logging only writes, not reads of sensitive records.
  • Storing full prompts and results with personal data in plain text.
  • Logs that admins can edit or delete.
  • No link between audit events and the conversation that caused them.

How to answer in the interview

"For every tool call I record who: the authenticated user, the client application, the agent or session, and on whose behalf. And what: the server, tool name and version, arguments, outcome, and identifiers of records read or changed, plus timestamps and any human approval with the approver. In regulated settings, reads matter as much as writes, because access itself may need justification. Sensitive fields are redacted or tokenised rather than stored in clear. Logs go to a central tamper-resistant store, immutable storage or a SIEM, with retention matching regulation, and they link to the conversation trace so investigators can reconstruct why the agent did something."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Balance audit with privacy? Log identifiers, not content; restrict and audit access to detailed payloads.
  • Retention period? Set by regulation and data class, agreed with compliance.
  • Who reviews the logs? Automated anomaly detection plus periodic compliance review.
