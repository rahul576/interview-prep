How do you measure the business value and ROI of an AI agent?

AI Agents & AIOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Measure an agent's ROI as value delivered minus full cost, against a baseline of the process before the agent, using outcome metrics (tasks completed correctly, time saved, throughput) rather than usage, and controlled comparisons wherever possible.

Detailed explanation

Usage numbers are seductive: thousands of conversations or agent runs per week look like success. But usage only shows that people tried the agent. Value comes from outcomes: tasks finished without human intervention, finished correctly, and finished faster or cheaper than before. A heavily used agent that produces drafts people rewrite, or answers customers must re-ask, can have negative value.

So the first step is a baseline: average handling time per ticket, cost per task, error rate, backlog and throughput per person before launch. Without it, every later number is a guess. After launch you track the automation (containment) rate, quality through audits, CSAT or defect rates, time saved per task and throughput per person.

The cost side must be complete: model tokens and infrastructure, development and maintenance, evaluation work, and the human oversight time for approvals and reviews. Add the cost of errors: rework, refunds, escalations and incidents the agent caused. Finally, self-reported productivity gains are often inflated, so use A/B tests between teams, staggered rollouts or before/after comparisons adjusted for seasonality.

How it works

  1. Define the process and its baseline metrics before launch.
  2. Agree the success metric with the business owner (for example cost per resolved ticket).
  3. Instrument the agent: completion, escalation, quality samples, time per task.
  4. Capture every cost: tokens, infrastructure, engineering, oversight, error remediation.
  5. Compare with a control group or a seasonally adjusted baseline.
  6. Report ROI monthly, since quality and cost change as the agent improves.

Example

A support triage agent, measured over one quarter:

Baseline:  12 min handling time/ticket, 40,000 tickets/quarter, £0.60 per agent-minute
Agent:     55% fully resolved, audited quality 94%, handling time on the rest 9 min
Value:     time saved ≈ 40,000 × (0.55×12 + 0.45×3) min ≈ 318,000 min ≈ £190,800
Costs:     tokens + infra £18,000, engineering £45,000, reviews £12,000, rework £9,000
ROI:       (190,800 − 84,000) / 84,000 ≈ 127%
Control:   region B without agent: handling time unchanged → gain is attributable

Common mistakes

  • Reporting usage (sessions, messages) as value.
  • Ignoring oversight and rework costs, which can erase the savings.
  • No baseline, so improvements cannot be proven.
  • Trusting survey-based "hours saved" without measured data.
  • Measuring once at launch instead of tracking over time.

How to answer in the interview

"I start with a baseline of the process before the agent: time per task, cost, error rate and throughput. After launch I measure outcomes, not usage: what share of tasks the agent completes without help, how good those completions are through audits or customer satisfaction, and how much time and throughput change. On the cost side I count everything, including model and infrastructure costs, engineering effort, the human time spent reviewing and approving, and the cost of mistakes. Where I can, I compare against a control group or a staggered rollout, because self-reported gains are often inflated. ROI is value minus full cost, and I track it over time."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why can heavy usage still mean low value? People may use it but redo the work, or it handles only trivial tasks.
  • How do you value time saved? Only as real value if it is redeployed: more throughput, smaller backlog or lower hiring.
  • What if the task has no baseline? Measure manually on a sample for a few weeks before launch.
