Describe a good staging-to-production promotion gate for a model, and who may override it.

MLOps · concept · levels senior, lead

In one line

A good promotion gate combines offline evaluation against pre-agreed thresholds, comparison with the current production model overall and by segment, smoke tests on golden inputs in the serving container, latency and payload checks under production-like load, and fairness checks where required; overrides need a named approver, documented reason and audit trail.

Detailed explanation

The gate exists so nobody ships a model on enthusiasm. Its parts:

Pre-agreed thresholds. Metrics and minimum values are set before training, on a fixed, versioned test set, so nobody moves the goalposts after seeing results.

Champion comparison. The candidate is evaluated against the production model on the same data and on important segments. It must be at least as good, or the trade-off (for example, slightly lower recall for much lower latency) must be explicitly accepted.

Smoke test with golden inputs. A fixed set of inputs with known expected outputs is sent through the packaged model in its serving container. This catches packaging, preprocessing, tokeniser and dependency errors that offline evaluation in a notebook never sees.

Operational checks. Latency percentiles, memory, start-up time and payload sizes under production-like load.

Fairness and compliance. In regulated domains such as lending, insurance or hiring, compare error and approval rates across groups, and confirm documentation like the model card is updated.

Overrides must exist for emergencies, but only by named approvers (for example, the model owner plus a risk lead), with a written reason and blast-radius assessment, recorded in the registry's audit trail and reviewed afterwards. Frequent overrides signal either bad gates or bad discipline.

How it works

  1. Candidate registered in staging with evaluation results.
  2. Pipeline runs threshold and champion comparisons, overall and per segment.
  3. Serving container deployed to staging; golden-input smoke tests run.
  4. Load test checks latency, memory and payload limits.
  5. Fairness and documentation checks where required.
  6. Approval recorded; promotion moves the production alias.

Example

promotion_gate:
  test_set: s3://eval/credit/v7
  thresholds: {auc_min: 0.80, recall_at_5pct_fpr_min: 0.55}
  champion_comparison: {max_auc_drop: 0.0, segments: [new_customers, region_south]}
  smoke: {golden_inputs: tests/golden.jsonl, exact_match: true}
  load: {rps: 300, p99_ms_max: 60, memory_mb_max: 1500}
  fairness: {metric: fpr_ratio, groups: [age_band], min_ratio: 0.8}
  override: {approvers: [model-owner, risk-lead], reason_required: true}

Common mistakes

  • Thresholds chosen after looking at the candidate's results.
  • Evaluating the notebook model, not the packaged container.
  • Overrides by anyone with admin rights and no record.
  • Ignoring segment regressions hidden by a better average.

How to answer in the interview

"My gate has offline evaluation on a fixed test set against thresholds agreed before training, a comparison with the production model overall and on key segments, a smoke test of golden inputs through the actual serving container, and latency and memory checks under realistic load. In regulated areas there's a fairness check too. Overrides are possible for emergencies, but only by named approvers with a written reason, recorded in the registry's audit trail and reviewed afterwards."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What makes you suspect gates are bypassed too often? Override counts rising, the same approver every time, or incidents after overridden releases.
  • Who sets thresholds? Model owner with business and risk stakeholders, before training.
  • Should gates be fully automated? Automated checks, with human approval for high-risk models.
