Which metrics do you use to evaluate regression models?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

Use MAE for an easy-to-explain average error, RMSE when large errors are especially costly, R-squared to compare explained variance, and MAPE for percentage error, then always inspect residuals and errors per segment.

Detailed explanation

Mean absolute error (MAE) is the average of |actual - predicted|. It is in the original units ("we are off by 12 minutes on average") and is robust to a few outliers. Root mean squared error (RMSE) squares errors before averaging and takes the square root, so one miss of 60 minutes counts far more than six misses of 10 minutes. Use it when big misses hurt disproportionately, such as under-forecasting stock for a peak day.

R-squared is 1 - (residual sum of squares / total sum of squares): the share of variance in the target explained by the model compared with always predicting the mean. It is useful for comparing models on the same dataset, but it says nothing about whether the error is acceptable for the business, and it can be negative on test data if the model is worse than the mean. MAPE expresses error as a percentage of the actual value, which is intuitive for forecasting, but it explodes when actuals are near zero and penalises over- and under-prediction asymmetrically. Alternatives are sMAPE, WAPE (total absolute error divided by total actuals) or MASE.

The right metric is the one that mirrors the business cost of errors. If over-forecasting and under-forecasting cost different amounts, a quantile (pinball) loss may be better. Averages also hide problems, so I check residual plots for patterns (errors growing with the target, seasonality left unexplained) and break errors down by segment, such as region or product category.

How it works

  1. Hold out a validation or time-based test set.
  2. Compute MAE, RMSE and R-squared (and MAPE/WAPE for forecasts).
  3. Compare against a naive baseline, such as the mean or last week's value.
  4. Plot residuals against predictions and key features.
  5. Slice errors by important segments and look for the worst groups.

Example

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, r2_score

mae  = mean_absolute_error(y_true, y_pred)
rmse = np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_true, y_pred))
r2   = r2_score(y_true, y_pred)
wape = np.abs(y_true - y_pred).sum() / np.abs(y_true).sum()
print(f"MAE={mae:.1f} RMSE={rmse:.1f} R2={r2:.3f} WAPE={wape:.1%}")

Common mistakes

  • Reporting R-squared alone as proof the model is "good".
  • Using MAPE on data with zeros or tiny values.
  • Never comparing against a naive baseline.
  • Looking only at averages and missing a badly served segment.

How to answer in the interview

"I start with MAE because it's in business units and easy to explain, and RMSE when large misses are especially costly, since squaring penalises them more. R-squared helps compare models on the same data but doesn't tell me if the error is acceptable. For forecasting I might use MAPE or better WAPE, avoiding MAPE near zero. Then I always look at residuals and per-segment errors, because a good average can hide a region where the model is badly wrong."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why might MAE and RMSE rank models differently? A model with a few large errors can win on MAE but lose on RMSE.
  • Can R-squared be negative? Yes, on held-out data when the model is worse than predicting the mean.
  • What if over-prediction costs more than under-prediction? Use an asymmetric or quantile loss.
