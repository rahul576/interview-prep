What is specific about operating time series forecasting models in production?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Operating forecasts means backtesting with rolling origins, choosing global versus per-series models, frequent retraining, careful calendar, promotion and external regressor pipelines, tracking WAPE/MASE and bias per segment, and publishing prediction intervals.

Detailed explanation

Evaluation: a single train/test split hides instability. Rolling-origin backtesting (time-series cross-validation) trains on data up to cut-off T, forecasts the next horizon, moves T forward and repeats across many cut-offs, matching how the model is used. Never shuffle; respect gaps between data availability and forecast date.

Scale: retailers forecast millions of series (product by store by day). Global models (one LightGBM or deep model across all series with series features) share patterns, help cold-start and sparse series, and are one artefact to operate. Per-series models (ARIMA, ETS per item) can fit idiosyncrasies but multiply training, monitoring and failure handling. Hierarchical reconciliation keeps store, region and total forecasts consistent.

Retraining: new observations arrive daily, so models retrain or refit frequently; pipelines must handle late data, backfills and restatements.

Features: holidays (moving ones like Easter or Diwali), promotions, price changes, weather and events are often more important than the model. These external regressors must be known for the forecast horizon; a missing promotion flag gives a terrible forecast. Pipelines need validation for future-known features.

Metrics: MAPE explodes for low-volume series (division by small actuals) and is undefined at zero, so prefer WAPE (sum of absolute errors over sum of actuals), MASE (scaled against a naive forecast) or pinball loss for quantiles. Track bias separately: systematic over-forecasting means excess stock. Report per segment (category, region, volume tier).

Uncertainty: planners need prediction intervals or quantile forecasts (P10, P50, P90) for safety stock decisions; check interval coverage.

Monitoring compares forecasts to actuals as they arrive, with alerts on accuracy and bias drops, plus a naive baseline as a sanity check.

How it works

  1. Build the feature pipeline including calendar, promotions and known future regressors.
  2. Backtest across rolling origins against naive and seasonal-naive baselines.
  3. Train a global model; publish quantile forecasts per series.
  4. Reconcile hierarchy levels if needed.
  5. Monitor WAPE, bias and interval coverage per segment as actuals land.
  6. Retrain on schedule; alert on regressions versus baseline.

Example

def backtest(df, cutoffs, horizon=28):
    rows = []
    for cutoff in cutoffs:
        train = df[df.date <= cutoff]
        test = df[(df.date > cutoff) & (df.date <= cutoff + pd.Timedelta(days=horizon))]
        model = fit_global_lgbm(train)
        pred = model.predict(make_features(test))
        wape = (test.y - pred).abs().sum() / test.y.sum()
        bias = (pred.sum() - test.y.sum()) / test.y.sum()
        rows.append({"cutoff": cutoff, "wape": wape, "bias": bias})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

Common mistakes

  • Random train/test splits on time series.
  • Using MAPE for intermittent demand.
  • Features that are not actually known at forecast time (leakage).
  • Reporting only one aggregate metric and missing bias.

How to answer in the interview

"Forecasting needs backtesting with rolling origins, retraining at many cut-offs, because one split hides instability. With thousands of series I usually prefer a global model across series, which handles sparse and new items better and is one thing to operate. Models retrain frequently. Calendar effects, promotions and external regressors need careful pipelines because a missing promotion flag ruins forecasts. I track WAPE or MASE plus bias per segment rather than MAPE, publish prediction intervals for planners, and monitor forecasts against actuals and a naive baseline."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why is MAPE problematic for low sellers? Small denominators inflate errors and zeros make it undefined; WAPE or MASE are more stable.
  • How do you handle new products? Global models with product attributes, or analogues from similar items.
  • What is interval coverage? The share of actuals falling inside the interval, which should match its nominal level.
