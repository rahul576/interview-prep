How do you set technical direction and engineering standards across multiple teams?

Leadership & Management · managerial · levels lead

In one line

Set direction with a small set of architecture principles and a tech radar built with teams, use a lightweight RFC/ADR process for significant decisions, make the right way easy with a paved road, balance autonomy and alignment (strict on interfaces, security and operability), and spread knowledge through guilds.

Detailed explanation

Across several teams, the risk is either chaos (every team picks its own stack, nothing interoperates, people cannot move between teams) or bureaucracy (a central committee approves everything and slows delivery). The interviewer wants a leader who creates alignment through influence and good defaults, not mandates.

Principles: a short list (five to ten) that guides decisions, for example "APIs first and backward compatible", "services own their data", "observable by default", "prefer managed services", "security is designed in". Principles are written with input from teams and explained with reasons.

Tech radar (popularised by Thoughtworks): technologies placed in Adopt, Trial, Assess, Hold rings, reviewed periodically. It tells teams what is supported and what to avoid, while leaving room for experimentation in Trial.

RFC and ADR process: significant or cross-team decisions are written as RFCs open for comment from anyone, reviewed in an open design review, and recorded as ADRs. Keep it lightweight: a template, clear criteria for when an RFC is needed (cost to reverse, cross-team impact), and time-boxed review.

Paved road (golden path): standards stick when compliance is the easiest option: service templates, shared libraries, CI/CD pipelines, default dashboards and security scanning provided by a platform team. Teams can leave the road but then own the extra cost.

Autonomy vs alignment: be strict on the seams (API standards, authentication, logging and tracing formats, data protection, on-call readiness) and flexible inside services. Guilds/communities of practice (backend, front end, testing) evolve standards bottom-up, and leads model the behaviour.

How it works

  1. Assess the current landscape and pain points with teams.
  2. Draft principles and a radar collaboratively; publish them.
  3. Introduce an RFC template and open design reviews.
  4. Fund a paved road: templates, pipelines, libraries.
  5. Define the non-negotiables at service boundaries.
  6. Run guilds; review radar and principles every six months.

Example

# ADR-017: Use OpenTelemetry for tracing in all services
Status: Accepted (2026-03-12)
Context: Three tracing libraries in use; cross-service traces break at team boundaries.
Decision: All services emit traces via the OpenTelemetry SDK/agent using W3C Trace Context.
          The service template includes it by default.
Consequences: + end-to-end traces, vendor-neutral; - migration effort (~2 days/service),
          tracked per team; legacy libraries moved to "Hold" on the tech radar.

Common mistakes

  • Imposing standards top-down without explanation or input.
  • A heavy review board that becomes a bottleneck.
  • Standards without tooling, so following them is extra work.
  • Controlling internal implementation details instead of interfaces.
  • Never revisiting the radar as technology moves.

How to answer in the interview

"I write down a small set of architecture principles and keep a tech radar of adopt, trial, assess and hold, built with input from teams rather than imposed. Significant decisions go through a lightweight RFC process with open design reviews, recorded as ADRs. Standards stick best when the right way is the easy way, so I invest in a paved road of templates, shared libraries and platform tooling. I'm strict on interfaces, security and operability, and flexible inside services. Guilds spread knowledge and evolve the standards over time."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Team wants a language nobody else uses? Weigh benefit vs hiring, support and on-call cost; allow as a Trial with explicit ownership.
  • How do you enforce standards? Automation in pipelines (linters, policies), templates, and review of exceptions.
  • How do you measure success? Adoption of the paved road, incidents at boundaries, lead time, team feedback.
