What is overfitting, and which techniques prevent it?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid, senior

In one line

Overfitting is when a model learns noise and quirks of its training data and fails on new data, detected by a gap between training and validation performance; prevent it with regularisation (L1/L2), dropout and early stopping, more data and augmentation, simpler models, and cross-validation.

Detailed explanation

A model's job is to learn patterns that generalise to data it has never seen. Overfitting happens when it instead memorises specifics of the training set, including random noise and coincidences: a fraud model that learns a particular merchant ID was always fraud in training, or an image model that recognises the watermark on photos of one class. It looks excellent in training and disappoints in production.

Detection: compare training and validation metrics. A large gap, for example 99% training accuracy and 80% validation accuracy, is the classic sign. Plotting both losses over training epochs shows validation loss starting to rise while training loss keeps falling.

Prevention techniques:

  • Regularisation adds a penalty for complexity to the loss. L2 (ridge, weight decay) shrinks weights smoothly; L1 (lasso) pushes some weights to exactly zero, also acting as feature selection.
  • Dropout randomly disables neurons during training, so the network cannot rely on specific paths.
  • Early stopping halts training when validation loss stops improving.
  • More data and data augmentation (rotations, crops, noise for images; paraphrases for text) make memorisation harder.
  • Simpler models: fewer features, limited tree depth, minimum samples per leaf, pruning.
  • Ensembles such as random forests average out variance.
  • Cross-validation gives a more reliable estimate when choosing models and hyperparameters.

Also watch for data leakage, where information from the target sneaks into features; it looks like great performance but is a related failure.

How it works

  1. Split data into training, validation and test sets.
  2. Train and monitor training versus validation metrics.
  3. If the gap grows, add regularisation, dropout or constraints.
  4. Use early stopping on validation loss.
  5. Tune with cross-validation; confirm once on the test set.

Example

import tensorflow as tf
model = tf.keras.Sequential([
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(128, activation="relu", kernel_regularizer=tf.keras.regularizers.l2(1e-4)),
    tf.keras.layers.Dropout(0.3),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid"),
])
model.compile(optimizer="adam", loss="binary_crossentropy", metrics=["AUC"])
model.fit(X_train, y_train, validation_data=(X_val, y_val), epochs=100,
          callbacks=[tf.keras.callbacks.EarlyStopping(patience=5, restore_best_weights=True)])

Common mistakes

  • Evaluating on the training data.
  • Tuning many hyperparameters against one small validation set.
  • Confusing data leakage with a genuinely great model.
  • Augmentations that change the label meaning (flipping digits like 6 and 9).

How to answer in the interview

"Overfitting is when a model learns noise and quirks of the training data instead of the general pattern, so it does great in training and poorly on new data. I detect it from the gap between training and validation performance. To prevent it I use regularisation, L2 to shrink weights or L1 to zero some out; dropout and early stopping for neural networks; more data and augmentation; simpler models or limits like tree depth; and cross-validation to choose models reliably. I'd also check for data leakage, which can look like overfitting's opposite."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Overfit the validation set? Yes, by tuning repeatedly on it; keep a final untouched test set.
  • L1 vs L2? L1 gives sparse weights; L2 shrinks all smoothly.
  • Overfitting in LLM fine-tuning? Watch validation loss; use few epochs and early stopping.
