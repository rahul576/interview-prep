How do you develop and grow engineers on your team?

People Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Grow engineers through career conversations, stretch assignments with real ownership, frequent specific feedback, a clear career ladder, and mentoring and sponsorship, with protected learning time.

Detailed explanation

Developing people is one of a manager's core responsibilities, and it also drives retention and team capability. Growth is personal: one engineer wants deep technical expertise, another wants to become a tech lead or manager, another wants to move into a new area such as security or data. So it starts with career conversations, separate from day-to-day status updates, where you learn what each person wants and turn it into concrete growth goals.

Most growth comes from experience, not courses. The classic guideline is roughly 70 percent learning on the job, 20 percent from others and 10 percent formal training. As a manager you create opportunities: stretch assignments such as leading a design, running an incident review, owning a migration or presenting to stakeholders. Give real ownership with a safety net: be available, but do not take the problem back.

Feedback should be frequent and specific, delivered in one-on-ones rather than saved for the annual review. A published career ladder makes expectations for the next level explicit, so promotion is about demonstrated scope and impact, not visibility or tenure. Connect people with mentors for advice, and act as their sponsor: speak up for them in promotion committees and put their names forward for visible work. Protect some learning time, for example conference talks, training budgets or a few hours each sprint.

How it works

  1. Hold quarterly career conversations: aspirations, strengths, gaps.
  2. Write two or three growth goals tied to the career ladder.
  3. Find stretch work in the roadmap that matches those goals.
  4. Give frequent feedback in one-on-ones; review progress monthly.
  5. Arrange mentors; sponsor people in calibration and promotion discussions.
  6. Celebrate growth publicly; review and reset goals.

Example

Example growth plan for a mid-level engineer aiming at senior:

goal: Operate at senior level within 12 months
gaps: [system design ownership, cross-team influence]
actions:
  - Lead design doc for the notifications rewrite (Q1)
  - Run two incident postmortems as facilitator
  - Mentor the new graduate hire
  - Present the design at the architecture forum
support: weekly 1:1 check-in, staff engineer as mentor
evidence_for_promo: design doc, postmortem actions closed, peer feedback

Common mistakes

  • Treating one-on-ones only as status meetings.
  • Giving feedback only at review time.
  • Growth plans with no real opportunities attached.
  • Taking problems back when stretch work gets hard.
  • Confusing mentoring (advice) with sponsorship (advocacy).

How to answer in the interview

"I start with regular career conversations to understand what each person wants, whether that is technical depth, leadership or a new area, and we turn it into two or three concrete goals mapped to our career ladder. Most growth comes from stretch assignments with real ownership, like leading a design or an incident review, where I stay available but do not take the problem back. I give frequent, specific feedback in one-on-ones rather than saving it for reviews. I connect people with mentors, act as a sponsor in promotion discussions, and protect some learning time. I know it is working when people take on bigger scope and get promoted."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you handle someone who wants a promotion but is not ready? Be honest, show the gaps against the ladder, and agree a plan with evidence and timelines.
  • How do you grow someone who wants to stay a strong IC? Give technical depth and influence, such as owning architecture or a domain.
  • How do you measure development? Promotions, scope increase, peer feedback and retention.
