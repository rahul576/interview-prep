What are model cards and model governance, and how do they work in practice?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

A model card documents a model's purpose, intended and out-of-scope uses, training data, performance overall and by segment, fairness results, limitations and owners; model governance is the process around it—inventory, risk classification, proportional approvals, monitoring, periodic review and audit—and it only works if the documents are actually read and change decisions.

Detailed explanation

Model cards were proposed by Mitchell et al. (2019) as short, standardised documentation for each model. A useful card covers: intended use and users; uses it must not be applied to; training data sources, dates and known gaps; evaluation metrics overall and across important segments; fairness results; known limitations and failure modes; ethical considerations; version, owner and contact. Hugging Face model cards and cloud registries popularised the format.

Model governance is broader. It starts with an inventory: every model in production with owner, purpose, risk classification, status and dependencies. Approvals are proportional to risk: a recommendation widget needs light review, while a credit model needs independent validation, as banks do under model risk management guidance such as the US SR 11-7 or the UK PRA's SS1/23. Regulations like the EU AI Act require documentation, risk management, human oversight and logging for high-risk AI systems. Governance also defines monitoring requirements, periodic reviews (for example annual revalidation), change management for retrains, and an audit trail.

The difference between good and bad governance is whether it is used. In a good setup, a risk committee reads the card, challenges limitations and sometimes blocks or constrains a model. In a bad one, a template is filled in after deployment to tick a box.

I automate as much as possible: generate the card's metrics, data lineage and approvals from the registry and pipeline, and keep the human-written parts short.

How it works

  1. Register each model in the inventory with owner and risk tier.
  2. Generate a card draft from pipeline metadata (data, metrics, slices).
  3. Owner adds intended use, limitations and ethical notes.
  4. Review and approval proportional to risk tier.
  5. Monitor per requirements; card updates on every retrain.
  6. Periodic review; retire models formally.

Example

# Model card: credit-risk v21
Intended use: pre-screening personal loan applications in India, ages 21-65
Out of scope: business loans, applicants with no credit history (use manual review)
Data: bureau + application data, Jan 2023 - Jun 2026
Performance: AUC 0.81 overall; 0.77 for first-time borrowers
Fairness: approval-rate ratio by gender 0.94; FPR ratio by age band 0.88
Limitations: degrades for incomes above 50 lakh (few samples)
Risk tier: high | Validator: Model Risk team | Owner: credit-ds@

Common mistakes

  • Writing the card after deployment as a formality.
  • Cards that never get updated after retraining.
  • One heavy approval process for every model regardless of risk.
  • No inventory, so nobody knows how many models are in production.

How to answer in the interview

"A model card is a short structured document per model: intended use and what it must not be used for, training data, metrics overall and by segment, fairness results, limitations and owners. Governance is the process around it: an inventory of production models with owners and risk tiers, approvals proportional to risk with independent validation for high-risk models, monitoring requirements, periodic reviews and an audit trail. The real test is whether a risk committee actually reads it and changes decisions. I generate as much as possible automatically from the registry and pipelines."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you keep cards current as models retrain? Generate metric sections in the pipeline and require owner sign-off on each new version.
  • What does the EU AI Act add? Mandatory documentation, logging and oversight for high-risk systems.
  • What goes in an inventory? Owner, purpose, risk tier, version, data sources, consumers and review dates.
