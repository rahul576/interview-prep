How does distillation work for LLMs, and when is it worth doing?

LLMOps & RAG · concept · levels senior, lead

In one line

In distillation, a large teacher model generates outputs for many realistic inputs and a small student is fine-tuned on them, reproducing the teacher on that narrow task at a fraction of the cost and latency; it pays off for high-volume, stable tasks, needs many high-quality, filtered examples, requires checking provider terms, and must be evaluated against the teacher with a fallback for low-confidence cases.

Detailed explanation

Classic knowledge distillation trained students on the teacher's probability distributions (soft labels). With API-only teachers you usually do sequence-level distillation: the teacher's generated outputs, sometimes including step-by-step reasoning, become supervised fine-tuning data for the student.

When it is worth it: the task is narrow and stable (extract fields from invoices, classify support tickets, summarise call notes in a fixed format, route requests), volume is high (hundreds of thousands to millions of calls per month), and latency or cost of the teacher is a real problem, or the model must run on-premises or on edge devices. For open-ended chat, distilled students fall short quickly outside the training distribution.

Data matters most. Sample real inputs broadly, including edge cases and multiple languages. Generate teacher outputs, ideally with the best prompt and even a reasoning mode, then filter errors: validate formats, use a judge, or have humans check a sample, because the student will faithfully learn the teacher's mistakes. Tens of thousands of examples are common for robust students.

Legal check. Some providers' terms restrict using outputs to develop competing models; review the terms for your provider and use case, and the licence of the student's base model.

Operate it. Evaluate the student against the teacher (and ground truth) on held-out data per segment; deploy with a confidence threshold that falls back to the teacher for hard cases; monitor agreement on a sample of traffic and retrain when input distribution drifts or the teacher improves. Some platforms offer managed distillation workflows that wire these steps together.

How it works

  1. Define task and success metrics; collect representative inputs.
  2. Teacher generates outputs (best prompt, possibly reasoning).
  3. Filter and validate outputs; build dataset.
  4. Fine-tune student (often LoRA on a small open model).
  5. Evaluate student vs teacher; set fallback threshold.
  6. Deploy; monitor agreement; retrain on drift.

Example

Invoice extraction, 3M documents/month
Teacher (frontier model): 97.8% field accuracy, $0.012/doc, 4 s
Student (8B fine-tuned, self-hosted): 97.1% accuracy, $0.0009/doc, 0.4 s
Fallback: confidence < 0.85 (about 4% of docs) -> teacher
Blended: 97.6% accuracy at about 11% of the original cost

Common mistakes

  • Distilling an open-ended task that keeps changing.
  • No filtering of teacher errors.
  • Ignoring provider terms of service.
  • No fallback path for out-of-distribution inputs.

How to answer in the interview

"In distillation a large teacher model produces outputs for lots of realistic inputs, and those become training data for a much smaller student fine-tuned to reproduce the teacher on that task. The student then runs at a fraction of the cost and latency, even self-hosted or on edge devices. It needs many high-quality examples covering real inputs, filtered for teacher mistakes, and works best for narrow, stable, high-volume tasks like invoice extraction or ticket classification. I evaluate the student against the teacher on held-out data, keep a fallback to the teacher for low-confidence cases, and check the provider's terms on using outputs for training."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Retraining signals? Falling agreement with teacher samples, more fallbacks, drift in input clusters.
  • Distil reasoning? Train on teacher rationales plus answers; can improve small models on structured reasoning.
  • Versus fine-tuning on human labels? Teacher labels are cheaper and scalable; humans validate.
