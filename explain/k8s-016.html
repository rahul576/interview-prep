What are topology spread constraints, and how do they differ from pod anti-affinity?

Kubernetes · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Topology spread constraints distribute matching pods evenly across domains defined by a topologyKey (zone, node), with maxSkew as the allowed imbalance and whenUnsatisfiable choosing DoNotSchedule (hard) or ScheduleAnyway (soft); pod anti-affinity only says "not together", which doesn't balance and doesn't scale to many replicas.

Detailed explanation

High availability needs replicas in different failure domains. If a scheduler packs six of nine replicas into one zone, a zone outage removes two thirds of capacity.

A spread constraint has:

  • topologyKey: a node label defining domains, such as topology.kubernetes.io/zone or kubernetes.io/hostname.
  • labelSelector: which pods to count (usually the app's own label); matchLabelKeys such as pod-template-hash counts only pods of the same rollout revision.
  • maxSkew: maximum allowed difference between the domain with the most and the fewest matching pods. With 3 zones and 9 replicas, maxSkew 1 enforces 3/3/3, tolerating 4/3/3 during changes.
  • whenUnsatisfiable: DoNotSchedule keeps the pod Pending rather than break the rule; ScheduleAnyway treats it as a scoring preference.
  • minDomains (with DoNotSchedule) requires a minimum number of eligible domains, useful with autoscalers that create new zones' nodes on demand.

Pod anti-affinity expresses only pairwise exclusion: "don't schedule me where a pod with label X runs". Required anti-affinity per hostname means at most one replica per node, so 20 replicas need 20 nodes and scale-ups go Pending. Preferred anti-affinity is just a score; it gives no guarantee of even distribution. Anti-affinity is also more expensive for the scheduler at scale.

A common production pattern is two constraints: spread by zone with maxSkew 1 (often DoNotSchedule) and by hostname with ScheduleAnyway. Combined with a PodDisruptionBudget, node drains and zone failures only ever remove a bounded share of replicas. Cluster-level default constraints can also be configured in the scheduler.

How it works

  1. Scheduler counts matching pods per domain of the topologyKey.
  2. For each candidate node, computes the skew if the pod lands there.
  3. Filters out nodes that would exceed maxSkew (hard) or scores them lower (soft).
  4. Places the pod; counts update for the next pod.
  5. Scale-down and later changes are not rebalanced automatically.

Example

spec:
  topologySpreadConstraints:
  - maxSkew: 1
    topologyKey: topology.kubernetes.io/zone
    whenUnsatisfiable: DoNotSchedule
    labelSelector: { matchLabels: { app: orders } }
    matchLabelKeys: [pod-template-hash]
  - maxSkew: 1
    topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname
    whenUnsatisfiable: ScheduleAnyway
    labelSelector: { matchLabels: { app: orders } }

Common mistakes

  • Required per-node anti-affinity for large replica counts.
  • labelSelector not matching the pod's own labels, so nothing is counted.
  • Hard zone spreading while the node pool exists in only two zones.
  • Expecting the scheduler to rebalance after scale-down.

How to answer in the interview

"Topology spread constraints tell the scheduler to spread matching pods evenly across domains given by a topologyKey, like zone or hostname. maxSkew is the allowed difference between the most and least loaded domain, and whenUnsatisfiable decides between refusing with DoNotSchedule or just preferring balance with ScheduleAnyway. Anti-affinity only says don't put these together: required anti-affinity caps replicas at the number of nodes, and preferred doesn't really balance. I usually spread by zone, hard, and by node, soft, plus a PDB, so a zone failure or upgrade removes only a fraction of replicas."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why unbalanced after scale-down? ReplicaSets choose which pods to delete without considering spread; use the descheduler to rebalance.
  • What does matchLabelKeys solve? Counting only the current revision during rolling updates.
  • Are constraints evaluated at runtime? No, only at scheduling time.
