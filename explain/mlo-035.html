How do you make a model smaller or faster for production inference?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior, lead

In one line

Make models smaller and faster with quantisation (INT8, 4-bit), distillation into a smaller student, pruning, compilation to optimised runtimes (ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, OpenVINO), plus serving-side batching and hardware choice, always validating accuracy on the evaluation set and slices.

Detailed explanation

Quantisation stores weights, and often activations, in lower precision: INT8 or FP8 instead of FP32, or 4-bit weights for LLMs (GPTQ, AWQ). This cuts memory by 2x to 8x and often doubles throughput or better on hardware with low-precision kernels. Post-training quantisation with a calibration dataset is quick; quantisation-aware training recovers accuracy when PTQ loses too much. Dynamic INT8 quantisation of linear layers is a cheap first step for transformer models on CPU.

Distillation trains a smaller student model to imitate the outputs (soft probabilities) of a large teacher. DistilBERT, for example, kept most of BERT's quality with fewer layers and faster inference. It needs a training effort but gives big, lasting gains.

Pruning removes weights, attention heads or whole neurons and channels that contribute little. Unstructured sparsity only helps if the runtime exploits it; structured pruning (removing whole channels or layers) gives real speed-ups on ordinary hardware.

Optimised runtimes compile the graph: ONNX Runtime, NVIDIA TensorRT, Intel OpenVINO, or torch.compile. They fuse operations, choose hardware-specific kernels and reduce overhead.

Serving-side choices matter as much: dynamic batching on GPUs, sequence-length bucketing, caching, and hardware choice (CPU vs GPU vs inference accelerators).

Every optimisation is validated on the evaluation set and important slices, because accuracy loss is often uneven—rare classes or long inputs suffer first.

How it works

  1. Measure baseline latency, throughput, memory and accuracy.
  2. Try cheap wins: ONNX/TensorRT export, dynamic INT8 quantisation, batching.
  3. Validate accuracy overall and per slice.
  4. If needed, distil or prune and retrain.
  5. Benchmark on target hardware under realistic load.
  6. Register the optimised artefact as a new version with its metrics.

Example

import torch
from torch.ao.quantization import quantize_dynamic

q_model = quantize_dynamic(model, {torch.nn.Linear}, dtype=torch.qint8)   # CPU INT8

torch.onnx.export(model, sample_inputs, "classifier.onnx", opset_version=17,
                  dynamic_axes={"input_ids": {0: "batch", 1: "seq"}})
# then: ONNX Runtime with graph optimisations, or trtexec --onnx=classifier.onnx --fp16

Common mistakes

  • Checking only overall accuracy after quantisation.
  • Pruning unstructured weights and expecting speed-ups on dense hardware.
  • Benchmarking with batch size 1 on a laptop and extrapolating.
  • Forgetting tokenisation and preprocessing time.

How to answer in the interview

"I'd start with the cheap options: export to ONNX Runtime or TensorRT, which fuse operations, and quantise to INT8 or FP16, which often doubles throughput with little accuracy loss. If that's not enough, distillation trains a smaller student to imitate the big model and keeps most of the quality, and structured pruning removes channels or layers. On the serving side, dynamic batching and the right hardware matter as much. I validate every step on the evaluation set and slices, because accuracy loss is rarely uniform."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What would you try first for a slow BERT-style classifier? ONNX Runtime plus INT8 or FP16, then a distilled smaller model.
  • PTQ vs QAT? PTQ is fast with calibration; QAT trains with simulated quantisation for better accuracy.
  • Why does 4-bit work for LLMs? Weight-only quantisation with careful grouping preserves most quality while fitting memory.
