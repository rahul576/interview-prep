Who governs MCP today, and how does the specification evolve?

MCP Servers · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

MCP was created and open-sourced by Anthropic in November 2024, donated in December 2025 to the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF), a directed fund under the Linux Foundation co-founded with OpenAI and Block, and evolves through public Spec Enhancement Proposals (SEPs), with extensions and a feature lifecycle allowing change without breaking the core.

Detailed explanation

Origins. Anthropic released MCP in November 2024 as an open standard to connect AI applications with tools and data, solving the "N models × M integrations" problem. Adoption was fast: OpenAI added MCP support to its Agents SDK and products in 2025, Google, Microsoft (VS Code, Windows, Copilot Studio) and AWS followed, and IDEs and developer tools such as Cursor, JetBrains and Zed adopted it. Thousands of servers appeared.

Neutral governance. A protocol controlled by a single vendor is a risk for competitors and enterprises. In December 2025, Anthropic donated MCP to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation, co-founded with OpenAI and Block (which contributed their own agent projects), with major cloud and technology companies joining as supporting members. The foundation provides neutral stewardship, trademark and community management, while technical direction stays with the project's maintainers.

How the spec evolves. Changes are proposed as Spec Enhancement Proposals (SEPs), discussed publicly on GitHub, reviewed by core maintainers and topic working groups (transports, security and auth, agents and so on), and decided with documented rationale. Accepted changes ship in dated revisions, implemented by the official SDKs, with conformance testing helping implementations stay consistent.

Evolving without breaking. The extensions framework lets new capabilities, such as enterprise-managed authorization or MCP Apps, be developed and adopted outside the core, negotiated via capabilities. A feature lifecycle of active, deprecated and removed, with minimum deprecation windows, gives implementers predictable time to adapt.

How it works

  1. Anyone opens a SEP describing motivation, design and compatibility.
  2. Community and working-group discussion; sponsors from maintainers.
  3. Core maintainers accept, revise or reject.
  4. Accepted SEPs land in the next dated revision or as extensions.
  5. SDKs implement; conformance tests verify; deprecated features follow the lifecycle.

Example

SEP-XXXX: "Caching hints for list results"
  Motivation: clients refetch tools/list every turn
  Proposal:  ttlMs + cacheScope on list/read results
  Compatibility: optional fields; old clients ignore them
  Status: Draft -> In review -> Accepted -> Final (included in revision YYYY-MM-DD)

Common mistakes

  • Saying MCP is "Anthropic's proprietary protocol" today.
  • Assuming governance changes mean the spec changes unpredictably; the lifecycle prevents that.
  • Ignoring SEPs and discussions, then being surprised by deprecations.
  • Building on draft features in production without fallbacks.

How to answer in the interview

"Anthropic created and open-sourced MCP in November 2024, and it was quickly adopted by OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, AWS and many IDEs and tools. In December 2025 Anthropic donated it to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation co-founded with OpenAI and Block, with large tech companies as supporting members, so it's governed neutrally. The spec evolves through Spec Enhancement Proposals discussed publicly and decided by core maintainers and working groups, with official SDKs and conformance tests. Extensions let new capabilities develop outside the core, and a feature lifecycle with deprecation windows gives implementers time to adapt."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why does neutral governance matter to enterprises? It reduces vendor lock-in risk and makes long-term investment safer.
  • Can a company add private features? Yes, via extensions or _meta, without forking the core.
  • Where do you follow changes? The specification repository, SEPs and the changelog per revision.
