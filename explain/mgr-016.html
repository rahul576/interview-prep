How would you decide whether to build a capability in-house or buy a vendor solution?

Leadership & Management · managerial · levels lead

In one line

Build what differentiates you and buy commodities: compare total cost of ownership over several years and time to market, assess vendor risk (lock-in, security, compliance, viability), validate with a proof of concept against agreed criteria, consider team skills and capacity, and define an exit plan before signing.

Detailed explanation

Build-vs-buy decisions are strategic: building consumes scarce engineering capacity for years, while buying creates dependencies and recurring costs. The interviewer wants a structured, business-minded approach, not "engineers love to build" or "always buy".

Differentiation: the key question is whether the capability is core to your competitive advantage. A logistics company's routing optimisation may be core; its CI system, monitoring, identity provider, email delivery or payroll are commodities. Build what makes customers choose you; buy the rest. Tools like Wardley mapping help visualise this.

Total cost of ownership over three to five years: for building, include initial development, ongoing maintenance, on-call, security patching, upgrades, documentation and opportunity cost (what the team would otherwise deliver). Engineers routinely underestimate maintenance. For buying, include licences (and how they scale with users or volume), integration, customisation, training, and vendor management.

Time to market: a vendor can often be live in weeks; building may take quarters.

Vendor risk: lock-in (proprietary formats, data export), security and compliance (certifications like SOC 2 or ISO 27001, data residency, GDPR, DPAs), viability (financial stability, roadmap, support quality), and pricing changes.

Evaluation: agree weighted criteria with stakeholders first, shortlist two or three options (including open source with self-hosting, a middle path), run a time-boxed POC with real use cases, and check references. Consider team skills and capacity to integrate or operate it. Define an exit plan: data export, abstraction layers around the vendor API, contract terms.

How it works

  1. Define the problem, requirements and success criteria.
  2. Decide: core differentiator or commodity?
  3. Shortlist build, buy and open-source options.
  4. Compare 3-5 year TCO and time to market.
  5. Assess vendor risk, security and compliance.
  6. Run a POC against weighted criteria.
  7. Decide, document as an ADR, and define the exit plan.

Example

Situation: Our team proposed building an in-house feature-flag service because the existing config-file approach was slowing releases. Task: As engineering lead, I had to recommend build or buy. Action: We agreed criteria with product and security: targeting rules, audit logs, SDKs for Java and React, data residency in India, and cost. Feature flagging was clearly not our differentiator. I estimated building at about three engineer-months plus ongoing maintenance and on-call, versus a vendor's annual licence. We ran a two-week POC with two vendors and an open-source option (Unleash, self-hosted), testing real flags in one service. We wrapped flags behind the OpenFeature API so the provider could be swapped later. Result: We chose the open-source option for residency and cost, live in three weeks instead of a quarter, and the team avoided owning a critical service that had nothing to do with our product.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing build cost with licence cost but ignoring maintenance.
  • Building commodity tools because they are interesting.
  • Buying without checking security, compliance or data residency.
  • Letting one vendor's demo decide instead of agreed criteria.
  • No exit plan or abstraction, leading to painful lock-in.

How to answer in the interview

"The first question is whether the capability is a core differentiator or a commodity: we build what makes us different and buy commodities like monitoring, CI or identity. Then I compare total cost of ownership over three years, including engineering time to build and maintain, against licence and integration costs, plus time to market. I assess vendor risks: lock-in, security and compliance, and viability. I'd run a short proof of concept against agreed criteria, check our team's skills and capacity, and define an exit plan before signing."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Bought a tool a year ago and the team hates it? Gather concrete issues, check configuration and training, renegotiate or plan migration using the exit plan.
  • Open source as a third option? Free licence, but you own operations and upgrades; managed open source can balance both.
  • When would you build a commodity? When no vendor meets hard requirements like residency, scale or cost at your volume.
