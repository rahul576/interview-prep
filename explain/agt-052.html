How can AI assist in writing incident postmortems, and what must humans still do?

AI Agents & AIOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

AI can draft the postmortem timeline, summarise impact and actions and suggest contributing factors and similar incidents from chat, alerts, deploys and tickets; humans must verify facts, own the analysis, root causes and action items, and keep the blameless tone.

Detailed explanation

The slowest part of a postmortem is evidence gathering: scrolling through hundreds of chat messages, matching alert timestamps with deployments, and reconstructing who did what and when. That is exactly the kind of collation work LLMs are good at.

Connected to the incident channel, alerting history, deployment and change records, status page updates and ticket comments, an assistant can produce a timeline with timestamps and links, a summary of customer impact from SLO metrics and support tickets, a list of responder actions, and suggested contributing factors and similar past incidents. This often cuts the drafting effort from days to hours.

Humans must still do the parts that create learning. They verify the draft, because models can misattribute events, merge two separate actions, or invent causal links ("the deploy caused the outage") that were never established. They perform the actual analysis: why the system allowed the failure, why detection took so long, which defences were missing. They decide root causes and contributing factors and choose action items with owners and due dates. They also protect the blameless culture; generated text can easily say "engineer X caused the outage by running Y", which should be rewritten in terms of systems and processes.

How it works

  1. Incident closes; the tool collects chat, alerts, deploys, status updates and tickets for the window.
  2. LLM drafts timeline, impact summary and actions, each with source links.
  3. It lists candidate contributing factors and similar incidents, marked as suggestions.
  4. Incident owner reviews and corrects facts.
  5. The team runs the review meeting and writes analysis and action items.
  6. A final check for blameless wording before publishing.

Example

## Timeline (draft – verify)
- 14:03 payments-api v3.18 deployed (pipeline #9921) [link]
- 14:07 Alert CheckoutLatencyHigh fired [link]
- 14:12 @oncall acknowledged; joined #inc-2291 [link]
- 14:31 Rollback to v3.17 started [link]
- 14:38 Latency back within SLO [graph]
## Suggested contributing factors (unconfirmed)
- Retry config change increased load on payments-db
- Canary stage skipped for this service

Common mistakes

  • Publishing the AI draft with minimal review.
  • Letting the AI pick root causes and action items.
  • Feeding only chat, missing deployment and alert data.
  • Leaving names in blame-like sentences.

How to answer in the interview

"AI is very good at the tedious collection work. It can build a timeline from the incident channel, alerts, deployments, status page and tickets with timestamps and links, summarise impact and actions, and point to similar incidents. But humans must verify every fact, because the draft can misattribute events or invent causal links. The team still does the real analysis of why the system allowed the failure, decides root causes and owns the action items, and makes sure the language stays blameless. Used that way it shortens postmortems a lot without losing learning."

Likely follow-up questions

  • First data sources to connect? Incident chat, alerting history and deployment events.
  • Can AI suggest action items? Yes as ideas, but the team prioritises and assigns them.
  • How to check accuracy? Every timeline entry must link to its source.
