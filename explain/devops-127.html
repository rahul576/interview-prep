How do you write good alerts and avoid alert fatigue?

Observability & SRE · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Good alerts are actionable, based on user-facing symptoms (SLOs), routed by severity (page versus ticket), protected against flapping with burn rates and for durations, linked to runbooks, and regularly reviewed and pruned.

Detailed explanation

Alert fatigue happens when on-call engineers receive so many low-value notifications that they start ignoring them, and then miss the one that matters. It is a reliability risk, not just an annoyance. The fix starts with a simple rule: a page must require a human to act now. If nobody needs to act, it should be a ticket, a dashboard panel or nothing at all.

Symptoms over causes: users do not care that CPU is at 90%; they care that checkout is failing or slow. Alert on error rate and latency against the SLO, and keep cause metrics (CPU, memory, queue depth) on dashboards for diagnosis. The exception is clear leading indicators of imminent failure, such as a disk that will be full in four hours or a certificate expiring in seven days, which become tickets or low-urgency alerts.

Avoiding flapping: a threshold crossed for 20 seconds should not wake anyone. Use for durations, and for SLOs use multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts (from the Google SRE Workbook): page when the error budget is burning fast over both a long and a short window, ticket when it burns slowly. Alertmanager grouping, deduplication and inhibition collapse 50 pod alerts into one notification.

Hygiene: track pages per shift, the share that were actionable, and time to acknowledge. Review alerts in a weekly or per-rotation meeting, delete or tune noisy ones, and require a runbook and owner for every paging alert.

How it works

  1. Define SLOs per user journey (for example 99.9% of checkout requests succeed).
  2. Create burn-rate alerts: fast burn pages, slow burn creates a ticket.
  3. Label alerts with severity and team; Alertmanager routes them accordingly.
  4. Attach a runbook URL annotation to every page.
  5. Group and inhibit related alerts to avoid storms.
  6. Review alert statistics after each on-call rotation and prune.

Example

- alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
  expr: |
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[1h]))
      / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
    and
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[5m]))
      / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[5m]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
  for: 2m
  labels: { severity: page, team: payments }
  annotations:
    runbook_url: https://runbooks.example.com/checkout-errors

Common mistakes

  • Paging on CPU, memory or single pod restarts.
  • No runbook, so the responder has to guess.
  • Thresholds copied from a blog without looking at real data.
  • Never deleting alerts, so the list only grows.
  • Sending everything to one Slack channel nobody reads.

How to answer in the interview

"My rule is that every page must be urgent and actionable, with a runbook; if nobody needs to act right now, it's a ticket or a dashboard. I alert on symptoms that users feel, error rate and latency against the SLO, using multi-window burn-rate alerts so short spikes don't page but a fast budget burn does. Severity labels drive routing: pages go to PagerDuty, the rest to tickets. Alertmanager groups and inhibits related alerts. And we review alert statistics every rotation, deleting or tuning anything noisy, with the goal of only a couple of pages per shift."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Disk at 80%: page, ticket or nothing? Usually a ticket; page only on a predicted fill time of a few hours using predict_linear.
  • What is a good pages-per-shift target? Google suggests at most about two incidents per 12-hour shift.
  • How do you handle alerts during deployments? Silences or inhibition for planned work, and SLO alerts that tolerate short blips.
