How do you scale a relational database on AWS when it becomes the bottleneck?

Cloud (AWS) · design · levels senior

In one line

Scale a bottlenecked relational database in order of cost: fix queries and indexes with Performance Insights, cache hot data in ElastiCache, offload reads to read replicas, scale up or move to Aurora (Serverless v2, RDS Proxy), and finally partition or shard writes or move suitable data to DynamoDB or analytics stores.

Detailed explanation

Find the cause. Performance Insights (now part of CloudWatch Database Insights) shows database load by top SQL, waits, hosts and users. Most bottlenecks are a few queries: missing or wrong indexes, N+1 patterns from ORMs, full table scans, heavy reports running on the primary, lock contention, or too many connections. Use EXPLAIN, slow query logs and pg_stat_statements. This is the cheapest and often most effective fix.

Cache read-heavy, rarely changing data (catalogue, configuration, sessions, computed pages) in ElastiCache with cache-aside and TTLs. Even a modest hit ratio removes a large share of reads.

Read replicas take read traffic and reports off the primary. The application must route reads separately (reader endpoint, separate data source) and tolerate replica lag, keeping read-your-own-writes paths on the primary. Aurora replicas share storage and usually lag milliseconds.

Vertical scaling: move to a larger or newer instance class (Graviton, memory-optimised), faster storage (gp3 with provisioned IOPS, io2), or Aurora for its storage engine, up to 15 replicas, and Serverless v2 for spiky load. RDS Proxy smooths connection storms from Lambda or large fleets.

Write scaling eventually needs structural changes: table partitioning (PostgreSQL declarative partitioning) for large tables, sharding by tenant or customer across clusters, Aurora Limitless Database for managed horizontal scaling on PostgreSQL, or moving workloads that do not need relational features, such as sessions, events, carts or time series, to DynamoDB, and analytics to Redshift or a lake via zero-ETL. Asynchronous processing with queues can also flatten write peaks.

How it works

  1. Measure: Performance Insights, slow logs, CPU, IOPS, connections.
  2. Optimise the top queries and indexes.
  3. Add caching for hot reads.
  4. Route reads and reports to replicas.
  5. Scale up or migrate to Aurora; add RDS Proxy.
  6. Partition, shard or offload when writes still saturate.

Example

-- Find the heaviest queries (PostgreSQL)
SELECT query, calls, total_exec_time/1000 AS total_s, mean_exec_time AS mean_ms
FROM pg_stat_statements ORDER BY total_exec_time DESC LIMIT 10;

EXPLAIN ANALYZE SELECT * FROM orders WHERE customer_id = 42 ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 20;
CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY idx_orders_customer_created
  ON orders (customer_id, created_at DESC);

Common mistakes

  • Buying a bigger instance before looking at the queries.
  • Sending all reads to replicas and breaking read-your-own-writes.
  • Caching without invalidation, serving stale data indefinitely.
  • Sharding too early, adding huge complexity for a fixable index problem.

How to answer in the interview

"I start by finding what is expensive: Performance Insights shows top SQL and waits, and usually missing indexes, N+1 queries or heavy reports are the cause, which is the cheapest fix. Then I cache hot, rarely changing data in ElastiCache. Read replicas take reads and reports off the primary, as long as the app tolerates lag. The primary can scale up or move to Aurora for faster storage, more replicas and Serverless v2, with RDS Proxy smoothing connections. Beyond that, write scaling needs partitioning or sharding by tenant, or moving suitable data like sessions or events to DynamoDB or an analytics store."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What are the risks of reading from replicas? Stale reads, broken read-after-write, and lag spikes under heavy writes.
  • How do you choose a shard key? High cardinality, even load, and most queries scoped to one key, like tenant ID.
  • When is DynamoDB a better fit? Known access patterns at massive scale without complex joins.
