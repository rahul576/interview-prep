What is the AWS Well-Architected Framework?

Cloud (AWS) · concept · levels entry

In one line

The AWS Well-Architected Framework is AWS's set of best practices and review questions for cloud workloads, organised into six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimisation and sustainability, applied through reviews in the Well-Architected Tool and specialised lenses.

Detailed explanation

Architectural decisions involve trade-offs, and teams often optimise one dimension while unknowingly creating risk in another. The framework provides a shared vocabulary and checklist of questions, with design principles and best practices for each.

Operational excellence: run and improve systems; infrastructure as code, small reversible changes, observability, runbooks, learning from incidents. Security: identity with least privilege, traceability, protection of data in transit and at rest, layered defences, automated security controls and incident preparation. Reliability: recover from failure and meet demand; multi-AZ design, automatic recovery, horizontal scaling, tested backups, managing service quotas, and testing recovery procedures. Performance efficiency: select the right resource types and sizes, use managed and serverless services, experiment, and monitor as needs change. Cost optimisation: pay only for what you need; right-sizing, Savings Plans, Spot, turning off idle resources, measuring cost per feature with tags. Sustainability (added in 2021): minimise environmental impact through efficient utilisation, managed services, efficient code and data lifecycle policies.

The Well-Architected Tool in the console lets you define a workload, answer the questions with the team, identify high- and medium-risk issues, create an improvement plan and track milestones. Lenses extend it for specific domains: serverless, SaaS, machine learning, data analytics, financial services and others, and organisations can create custom lenses for their own standards. AWS Partners and Solutions Architects often run formal reviews.

The framework is guidance, not a certification. Its value comes from regular reviews (for example before launch and yearly) and acting on the findings.

How it works

  1. Define the workload and its business goals.
  2. Answer pillar questions with the architects and operators.
  3. Identify high-risk issues.
  4. Prioritise improvements by business impact.
  5. Implement, then record a milestone.
  6. Repeat periodically or after major changes.

Example

A review of an e-commerce workload finds: single-AZ RDS (reliability risk), no tested restores (reliability), IAM users with old access keys (security), no budgets or tagging (cost), manual deployments (operational excellence). Improvement plan for the quarter:

1. RDS Multi-AZ + monthly restore test         (Reliability, HRI)
2. Move people to IAM Identity Center, remove keys (Security, HRI)
3. Terraform + CI/CD for deployments           (Operational excellence)
4. Mandatory tags + AWS Budgets                 (Cost optimisation)
5. Graviton instances, S3 lifecycle rules       (Performance, Sustainability)

Common mistakes

  • Treating the review as a one-off compliance exercise.
  • Naming only five pillars (forgetting sustainability).
  • Trying to maximise every pillar instead of balancing trade-offs.

How to answer in the interview

"The Well-Architected Framework is AWS's set of best practices and questions for designing and running workloads, organised in six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimisation and sustainability. Teams use the Well-Architected Tool to review a workload, find high-risk issues and track an improvement plan, and there are lenses for areas like serverless or SaaS. I use it as a structured checklist before launch and periodically afterwards, focusing on the risks that matter most to the business rather than trying to perfect every pillar at once."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which pillar first for a startup? Security and cost basics first, then reliability as customers depend on it.
  • What is a lens? Domain-specific questions and practices, such as the serverless lens.
  • How do pillars conflict? More reliability (multi-region) increases cost; you balance by business need.
