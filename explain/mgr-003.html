The product manager wants a feature in two weeks, but your team estimates six. How do you handle it?

Stakeholder Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Understand the real goal and deadline driver, then present options (MVP scope, phased delivery, extra help or a new date) with transparent trade-offs, never silently cut quality, and agree and document the decision.

Detailed explanation

A gap between "the business wants it in two weeks" and "engineering estimates six" is normal; the mistake is turning it into a fight or quietly agreeing and then failing. Your role as a lead is to turn a conflict into a decision with known trade-offs.

Start by understanding why two weeks. Is it a contractual customer commitment, a marketing launch, a regulatory date, or simply a hope? Also ask what problem the feature really solves. Often the core value can be delivered with a fraction of the scope.

Then check your own estimate: break it down, identify risks and uncertainties, and be clear about confidence. Present options rather than "no": a minimum viable product that solves the core problem by the date, phased delivery of the rest, temporarily reprioritising other work, adding people only if they can help immediately (Brooks's law warns that adding people to a late project often makes it later), or moving the date. For each option, explain impact on scope, risk and other commitments.

Be explicit that cutting testing, security or operability silently is not an option: it creates incidents and technical debt that cost more later. If a deliberate shortcut is accepted, record it as technical debt with a plan to pay it back. Finally, agree one option with the PM and relevant stakeholders and document it, so expectations are shared.

How it works

  1. Ask what drives the date and what outcome matters most.
  2. Re-check the estimate: breakdown, risks, confidence level.
  3. Identify the smallest valuable slice (MVP).
  4. Prepare two or three options with scope, risk and cost.
  5. Discuss trade-offs openly with the PM and stakeholders.
  6. Agree, document and track the decision.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a flat "no" without alternatives.
  • Agreeing to the date and hoping for the best.
  • Silently skipping tests or reviews to hit the date.
  • Assuming more people automatically means faster.
  • Not documenting the agreed trade-off.

How to answer in the interview

"First I would understand the real goal and what is driving the date: a customer commitment, a marketing event, or just a wish. Then I would double-check our estimate and present options instead of a flat no. For example, an MVP that solves the core problem in two weeks with the rest phased afterwards, pausing other work, or moving the date. I would explain the trade-offs and risks transparently, and be clear that silently cutting testing or adding hidden technical debt is not an option, because it costs more later. We would agree one option together with the stakeholders and document it, so everyone has the same expectations."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What if leadership insists on the full scope in two weeks anyway? Make risks explicit in writing, commit to best effort with checkpoints, and escalate the trade-off rather than burning out the team.
  • How do you make estimates more reliable? Break work down, use historical velocity, and give ranges with confidence.
  • How do you handle scope creep afterwards? Every new request goes through the same trade-off conversation.
