What is interpretability for large language models, and why does it matter?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Interpretability is understanding how a model internally produces its outputs; LLMs are largely opaque, mechanistic interpretability research finds features and circuits inside them, and since a model's written explanation may not reflect its real process, practical engineering relies on evaluations, tracing and citations.

Detailed explanation

For a linear model we can read the coefficients. For an LLM with billions of parameters across dozens of layers, we see inputs and outputs, but why a particular answer was produced is not directly readable from the weights. Individual neurons often respond to many unrelated concepts (polysemanticity), because models pack more concepts than they have neurons (superposition).

Mechanistic interpretability, pursued by Anthropic and other labs and academics, tries to reverse-engineer the computation. Techniques such as sparse autoencoders and dictionary learning decompose activations into more interpretable features, internal representations of concepts like a city, a programming bug or deception, some shared across languages. Circuit analysis and attribution graphs trace how features combine through layers to produce an output. Published work has shown, for example, models planning ahead (choosing a rhyme word before writing the line) and using shared multilingual concept representations. Researchers can also steer behaviour by amplifying or suppressing features. Probing and attention analysis are older, lighter tools.

An important practical lesson: when asked to explain itself, a model generates a plausible explanation, which may not be faithful to the computation that produced the answer. Research has found chain-of-thought sometimes omits factors that actually influenced the answer. So reasoning text is useful for debugging and often helps accuracy, but it is not a guaranteed explanation.

Why it matters: safety (detecting deception, hidden goals or harmful capabilities), debugging (why a model fails on certain inputs), trust and accountability in high-stakes uses, and potentially regulatory explanation requirements. In everyday engineering, full internal explanations aren't available, so we rely on behavioural evaluations, tracing of exactly what the model saw (prompts, retrieved documents, tool calls), citations to source documents users can check, and for regulated decisions, interpretable models or rules for the decision itself.

How it works

  1. Record internal activations while the model processes many inputs.
  2. Train sparse autoencoders to decompose activations into features.
  3. Label features by the inputs that activate them.
  4. Trace how features connect across layers into circuits for a behaviour.
  5. Validate by intervening: suppress or amplify features and observe output changes.

Example

Production "interpretability" toolkit for an LLM support assistant:
- Trace: prompt version, retrieved doc IDs, tool calls and outputs per request
- Citations: every answer links to the passages it used
- Evals: regression set of 300 questions with expected sources
- Attribution check: remove each retrieved passage, see if the answer changes
- Regulated decision (loan approval): made by a scorecard model, LLM only drafts the letter

Common mistakes

  • Treating chain-of-thought as a faithful explanation.
  • Assuming attention weights fully explain model decisions.
  • Claiming interpretability research is solved or production-ready for audits.
  • Letting an LLM make regulated decisions because it "explains its reasoning".

How to answer in the interview

"Interpretability means understanding how a model really arrives at an output. LLMs are largely opaque. Mechanistic interpretability research, from Anthropic and others, finds features, internal concept representations, and circuits, and has shown things like models planning ahead. A key lesson is that a model's own explanation of its reasoning may not reflect what actually happened inside, so I treat chain-of-thought as useful but not proof. It matters for safety, debugging and trust. Day to day, I rely on evaluations, tracing what the model saw and did, and citations."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why isn't a model's self-explanation enough for a regulated decision? It may be unfaithful and can't be audited against the real computation.
  • What is superposition? Models represent more concepts than neurons by overlapping them, making neurons hard to interpret.
  • What is feature steering? Changing behaviour by amplifying or suppressing an identified internal feature.
