Your team has missed the last three sprint commitments. How do you handle it?

Delivery & Execution · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Don't push harder; diagnose with data (unplanned work, estimate accuracy, blocked items, scope change), listen through retros and one-on-ones, address the real causes with concrete changes (smaller stories, WIP limits, protected focus, visible dependencies), and reset stakeholder expectations to a reliable forecast.

Detailed explanation

Three missed sprints is a systemic signal, not a motivation problem. The interviewer wants to see a manager who investigates before acting, involves the team, protects trust, and manages upwards honestly.

Diagnose: look at the last few sprints. How much unplanned work entered (support, incidents, urgent requests)? How did planned vs completed points or items compare, and was the gap in a few large stories? How many items were blocked waiting on other teams, environments or reviews? Did scope change mid-sprint? Is work in progress high, with many things started and few finished? Check also capacity changes: leave, attrition, onboarding.

Listen: run an honest retrospective focused on the system, plus one-on-ones where people can say what they would not say in a group: unclear requirements, pressure to over-commit, technical debt slowing everything down.

Act on causes: typical fixes include slicing stories smaller so they finish within days, WIP limits, a rotating support/interrupt person to protect others' focus, refinement sessions so stories are ready, making cross-team dependencies visible early, and committing based on historical throughput rather than optimism. Address technical debt that inflates every estimate.

Manage expectations: tell stakeholders openly what is happening and what you are changing, and switch to forecasts based on actual throughput with ranges. Track predictability (planned vs delivered) over the next sprints and adjust. Also question whether "sprint commitment" is being used as a fixed promise; Scrum now speaks of forecasts and a sprint goal.

How it works

  1. Gather data from the tracker for the last 3-5 sprints.
  2. Hold a retrospective and one-on-ones; listen without blame.
  3. Identify top two or three causes.
  4. Agree a small set of changes with the team.
  5. Communicate the situation and plan to stakeholders.
  6. Measure predictability and revisit after two sprints.

Example

Situation: I took over a team that had delivered around 55-60% of planned work for three sprints, and the product manager was losing trust. Task: Restore predictable delivery without burning out the team. Action: Data showed about 30% of capacity went to unplanned production support, and most carried-over items were large stories blocked on another team's API. In one-on-ones, two engineers said they felt pressured to accept whatever was asked in planning. We introduced a weekly support rotation, a rule that stories must fit in three days, a WIP limit of two per person, and planning based on the average of the last three sprints. I met the PM and the other team's lead to set up a shared dependency board. Result: Within three sprints we consistently delivered over 85% of the sprint goal, and the PM started using our forecasts in the roadmap.

Common mistakes

  • Demanding overtime or "more commitment".
  • Blaming individuals or the estimates alone.
  • Changing many things at once without measuring.
  • Hiding the problem from stakeholders.
  • Ignoring unplanned work and support load.

How to answer in the interview

"I wouldn't start by pushing harder. First I'd look at the data: how much unplanned work came in, how estimates compared with actuals, what was blocked by dependencies, and whether scope changed mid-sprint. Then I'd run an honest retro and one-on-ones and listen. Usually it's too much scope, interruptions, optimistic estimates or dependencies. I'd agree concrete changes like smaller stories, WIP limits, a rotating support person and visible dependencies. I'd be open with stakeholders, reset expectations to what we reliably deliver, and track predictability over the next sprints."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Stakeholder keeps adding work mid-sprint? Make the cost visible: swap something out, route requests through the PO, agree an urgent-work policy.
  • One person is the bottleneck? Pairing and knowledge sharing to spread skills.
  • Is velocity the right measure? Use it for planning only; prefer throughput and sprint-goal success.
