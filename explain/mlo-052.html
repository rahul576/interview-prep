How do you organise data labelling for ML, including human-in-the-loop and active learning?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Organise labelling with clear guidelines, quality control (multiple annotators, inter-annotator agreement, gold questions, expert adjudication), the right tools and workforce, active learning to label the most informative examples first, production feedback and human review as label sources, and model-assisted pre-labelling (including LLMs) verified by humans, with versioned labels.

Detailed explanation

Labels are often the biggest determinant of model quality, and the most underestimated cost.

Guidelines. A written labelling guide with definitions, many examples and explicit edge cases ("a refund request mentioning a delay is 'refund', not 'delivery'"). It evolves as annotators raise questions.

Quality control. Several annotators label an overlapping sample; measure inter-annotator agreement with Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha. Low agreement usually means the guidelines or the task definition are unclear, not lazy annotators. Gold questions with known answers, mixed in unseen, catch careless work. Disagreements go to expert adjudication, which also improves the guide.

Tools and workforce. Label Studio, CVAT, Prodigy, or cloud services such as SageMaker Ground Truth manage tasks, assignment and review. Workforce choice depends on sensitivity and expertise: in-house experts (doctors, lawyers, fraud analysts), managed vendors, or crowdsourcing for simple tasks. Sensitive data needs contracts, access control and secure tooling.

Active learning. The current model scores unlabelled data; examples where it is most uncertain (probabilities near 0.5, high entropy, disagreement between ensemble members) or most diverse are labelled first, often reaching target accuracy with far fewer labels than random sampling.

Production as a source. User feedback (thumbs up or down, corrections), human-in-the-loop review of low-confidence predictions, and downstream outcomes such as chargebacks all produce labels, though with selection bias.

Pre-labelling. Models, including LLMs, propose labels that annotators accept or correct, speeding work considerably, but humans must verify, and you should measure how often pre-labels are wrong to avoid anchoring bias. Version labels and guidelines like any dataset.

How it works

  1. Write guidelines; pilot with a small batch and measure agreement.
  2. Refine guidelines; set up gold questions and review.
  3. Choose tool and workforce by sensitivity and expertise.
  4. Bootstrap a model; use active learning to pick the next batch.
  5. Pre-label with a model or LLM; humans verify.
  6. Feed production reviews back; version every label set.

Example

import numpy as np
probs = model.predict_proba(unlabelled_X)             # shape (n, classes)
entropy = -(probs * np.log(probs + 1e-12)).sum(axis=1)
next_batch = unlabelled_ids[np.argsort(-entropy)[:500]]   # most uncertain first
send_to_label_studio(next_batch, prelabels=probs.argmax(axis=1))

Common mistakes

  • No written guidelines, so annotators interpret classes differently.
  • Never measuring agreement.
  • Accepting LLM labels without human verification.
  • Overwriting labels without versioning.

How to answer in the interview

"Good labels start with clear guidelines with edge-case examples, and quality control: overlapping samples to measure inter-annotator agreement, gold questions to catch careless work and expert adjudication of disagreements. Tooling like Label Studio and the workforce depend on sensitivity and expertise. Active learning cuts cost by labelling the examples the model is most uncertain about first. Production adds labels through feedback and human review of low-confidence cases. Model or LLM pre-labelling speeds things up, but humans verify, and labels are versioned."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you detect inconsistent or wrong labels? Agreement metrics, confident-learning tools like cleanlab, and reviewing high-loss examples.
  • What is anchoring bias in pre-labelling? Annotators accept suggested labels too readily.
  • Uncertainty vs diversity sampling? Uncertainty finds hard cases; diversity avoids redundant batches.
