How do you keep your technical skills up to date?

Closing · behavioral · levels entry, mid, senior, lead

In one line

Describe a deliberate learning system: curated sources (docs, release notes, books, courses), hands-on practice (side projects, prototypes, open source), and sharing (talks, write-ups, mentoring), with a recent concrete example.

Detailed explanation

Technology changes constantly: Java releases every six months, cloud services add features weekly, and AI-assisted development has changed daily work. Interviewers ask this to check curiosity, self-direction and judgement: can you keep up without chasing every trend, and does your learning actually reach your work?

A strong answer shows three layers. Inputs: a small, intentional set of sources, such as official documentation and release notes for your stack (JDK, Spring, Kubernetes, your cloud), a few high-quality engineering blogs or newsletters, conference talks, a book or course on a deeper topic each quarter. Practice: you learn by building: a small side project, a proof of concept at work, contributing to open source, solving a real problem with the new tool. Sharing: you teach through brown-bag sessions, internal docs or code reviews, which deepens understanding and multiplies impact.

Show prioritisation: you learn what matters for your role and the company's direction, not everything. Mention how you evaluate new tech critically (proof of concept, measuring trade-offs) before recommending it. Always have a specific, recent example ready, because the follow-up "what did you learn last month?" is almost guaranteed.

How it works

  1. Name your regular sources briefly (two or three).
  2. Explain how you turn learning into practice.
  3. Give one concrete recent example with an outcome.
  4. Show how you share knowledge with others.
  5. Mention how you choose what to learn.

Example

Situation: Our team was moving services to Java 21 and Spring Boot 3, and many of us had not used virtual threads. Task: I wanted to understand whether virtual threads could replace our reactive code in one I/O-heavy service. Action: I read the JEP and Spring documentation, then built a small benchmark comparing our WebFlux endpoint with a virtual-thread MVC version under Gatling load. I found a pinning issue caused by a synchronized block in an old client library and documented the workaround. Result: We migrated the service to plain MVC with virtual threads, which cut the code size noticeably and made onboarding easier with similar throughput. I gave a 30-minute internal talk, and two other teams used my benchmark setup.

Common mistakes

  • Listing twenty blogs and podcasts with no evidence of application.
  • Claiming to learn "everything" with no focus.
  • No recent example, or an example several years old.
  • Only mentioning work-mandated training.
  • Name-dropping trendy tech you cannot discuss in depth.

How to answer in the interview

"I keep a small set of sources: official release notes and docs for our stack, a couple of engineering blogs, and one book or course a quarter on something deeper. But I learn best by building, so I try new things in small prototypes or side projects. For example, when we moved to Java 21 I benchmarked virtual threads against our reactive service, found a pinning issue, and that led us to simplify the service. I shared it in an internal talk, and teaching it made sure I really understood it."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What did you learn in the last month? Have a specific, recent topic with one insight.
  • How do you decide whether new tech is worth adopting? Proof of concept, compare against the problem, consider team skills and maintenance.
  • How do you use AI tools in learning? As a starting point and pair, always verifying against documentation and tests.
