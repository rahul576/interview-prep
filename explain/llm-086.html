What should you check about licensing before using an open-weight model commercially?

LLMOps & RAG · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Open-weight is not the same as open source: before commercial use, read the exact licence of the specific model and derivative, checking commercial rights, acceptable use policies, user-count thresholds, rules on using outputs to train other models, attribution/naming requirements, then get legal review and track licences in an inventory.

Detailed explanation

"Open-weight" only means you can download the weights. Whether you may use them commercially, modify them or redistribute them depends entirely on the licence, and licences vary widely. Some models are released under permissive OSI-approved licences such as Apache 2.0 or MIT, which allow commercial use, modification and redistribution with minimal conditions (notices, and for Apache 2.0 a patent grant). Many popular models instead use custom community licences written by the vendor.

Custom licences commonly include: an acceptable use policy that forbids certain applications (and may be updated by reference); a scale threshold, for example needing a separate commercial licence above a very large number of monthly active users; attribution and naming requirements, such as displaying "built with" notices or including the original model name in derivative model names; restrictions on using outputs to train or improve other models, particularly competing ones; and sometimes field-of-use, regional or export restrictions. Some licences are non-commercial or research-only.

Two further points catch teams out. Derivatives inherit terms: a fine-tune you download from a hub is bound by its base model's licence and possibly its own additional terms, and the uploader's stated licence may simply be wrong. And training data provenance matters: some vendors document data sources, others do not, which affects IP and regulatory risk (for example transparency duties under the EU AI Act for general-purpose models).

How it works

  1. Identify the exact model, version and licence text (not the hub tag alone).
  2. Trace derivatives back to the base model and combine all terms.
  3. Check commercial use, user thresholds, AUP, output-use rules, attribution and redistribution.
  4. Escalate custom or unusual terms to legal review.
  5. Record the model, licence, version and obligations in an AI/software inventory.
  6. Re-check when you upgrade versions, since licences change between releases.

Example

# model-inventory.yaml
- model: example-8b-instruct
  source: vendor release, v3.1
  licence: Custom community licence v3.1
  commercial_use: allowed
  conditions:
    - "Separate licence above the stated MAU threshold"
    - "Display attribution notice in product docs"
    - "Derivative names must include base model name"
    - "Comply with acceptable use policy (linked)"
  legal_review: approved 2026-09-12
- model: example-7b
  licence: Apache-2.0
  conditions: ["Retain NOTICE file"]

Common mistakes

  • Assuming "open" or "on Hugging Face" means free for any commercial use.
  • Trusting the licence tag on a community fine-tune without checking the base model.
  • Using model outputs to train another model when the licence forbids it.
  • Not re-checking licences when upgrading to a new model version.

How to answer in the interview

"First, open-weight doesn't automatically mean open source. Some models are Apache 2.0 or MIT, which is straightforward. Many use custom community licences, so I check the exact licence version for commercial rights, the acceptable use policy, thresholds like a monthly active user limit above which you need a separate deal, attribution or naming requirements for derivatives, and restrictions on using outputs to train other models. For fine-tunes I trace back to the base model, because derivatives inherit those terms. Anything unusual goes to legal, and we track every deployed model and its obligations in an inventory, just like software dependencies."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do fine-tune licences relate to the base model? The fine-tune must comply with the base licence; it can add restrictions but cannot remove them.
  • Is Apache 2.0 risk-free? Mostly, but you still need notices, and data provenance and AI regulation still apply.
  • Who owns outputs? Usually the user under most licences, but check, and output-use restrictions may still apply.
