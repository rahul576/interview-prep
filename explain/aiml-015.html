How does gradient descent train a model, and what is the role of the learning rate?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

Gradient descent minimises a loss function by repeatedly moving parameters a small step opposite to the gradient; the learning rate sets that step size: too high diverges, too low crawls.

Detailed explanation

Training a model means finding parameters (weights) that make the loss, a number measuring how wrong predictions are, as small as possible. The gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters points in the direction of steepest increase, so we update θ ← θ − η · ∇L(θ), where η is the learning rate. Repeating this walks downhill on the loss surface.

The learning rate is usually the single most important hyperparameter. Too high and updates overshoot the minimum: the loss oscillates, spikes or becomes NaN. Too low and training takes forever or settles in a poor flat region. A practical approach is a learning-rate range test or trying values on a log scale (1e-1, 1e-2, 1e-3...).

Computing the exact gradient over the whole dataset (batch gradient descent) is expensive for large data. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) uses one example per step: cheap but noisy. Mini-batch gradient descent, with batches of, say, 32 to several thousand, is the standard: it uses GPU parallelism efficiently, and the noise helps escape sharp minima and saddle points. Momentum accumulates a moving average of gradients to smooth and accelerate progress. Adaptive optimisers such as Adam and AdamW scale the step per parameter using running estimates of gradient mean and variance. Learning-rate schedules, such as linear warmup followed by cosine decay, are standard when training transformers because large early steps destabilise training.

How it works

  1. Initialise parameters (randomly for neural networks).
  2. Take a mini-batch and compute predictions and the loss.
  3. Compute gradients (via backpropagation for networks).
  4. Update parameters: subtract learning rate times gradient (or the optimiser's adjusted step).
  5. Adjust the learning rate according to the schedule.
  6. Repeat for many steps or epochs, monitoring training and validation loss.

Example

import numpy as np

w, b, lr = 0.0, 0.0, 0.01
for epoch in range(1000):
    y_hat = w * X + b
    grad_w = 2 * np.mean((y_hat - y) * X)   # d(MSE)/dw
    grad_b = 2 * np.mean(y_hat - y)
    w -= lr * grad_w
    b -= lr * grad_b

Common mistakes

  • Keeping one fixed learning rate when a schedule or warmup would help.
  • Not scaling input features, which makes the loss surface elongated and slow to descend.
  • Changing batch size a lot without re-tuning the learning rate.
  • Watching only training loss and missing overfitting on validation.

How to answer in the interview

"Training minimises a loss function. Gradient descent computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter, which points uphill, and takes a small step the other way, over and over. The learning rate is the step size: too high and the loss bounces or explodes, too low and it barely moves. In practice we use mini-batches for efficiency and helpful noise, optimisers like Adam or SGD with momentum, and schedules such as warmup then cosine decay to keep training fast and stable."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What in a loss curve suggests the learning rate is too high? Oscillating or spiking loss, or NaN values.
  • Why does mini-batch noise help? It helps escape saddle points and sharp minima, often improving generalisation.
  • What does momentum do? Averages past gradients to dampen oscillation and speed up consistent directions.
