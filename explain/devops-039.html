Explain SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and error budgets. How do they influence release decisions?

Observability & SRE · concept · levels senior, lead

In one line

An SLI is a measured ratio of good events, an SLO is the internal target for it over a window, an SLA is a contract with penalties (looser than the SLO), and the error budget (100 percent minus SLO) governs releases: ship while budget remains, slow down or freeze when it's burning or exhausted, alerting on burn rate.

Detailed explanation

SLI (service level indicator): a quantitative measure of user experience, best expressed as good events / valid events: share of requests returning non-5xx in under 300 ms, share of successful checkouts, share of data processed within 10 minutes. Measure as close to the user as possible (load balancer or client), not CPU.

SLO (service level objective): a target for the SLI over a window, such as 99.9 percent over a rolling 30 days. It's an internal engineering and product agreement; 100 percent is never the right target, since it's unachievable and blocks all change, and users can't tell the difference beyond a point.

SLA (service level agreement): an external contract with customers, with consequences like service credits. It should be looser than the SLO (99.5 percent SLA with a 99.9 percent SLO) so you have warning before breaching it.

Error budget: 100 percent minus the SLO. At 99.9 percent over 30 days, 0.1 percent of requests may fail, or about 43 minutes of full downtime equivalent. The budget turns reliability into a shared, data-driven decision:

  • Budget healthy: ship features fast, run experiments, accept risk.
  • Burning fast or exhausted: per an agreed error budget policy, freeze risky releases, prioritise reliability fixes, add tests and automation, until the budget recovers.

This ends the dev-versus-ops argument: both sides agreed the number in advance.

Burn-rate alerting: burn rate is how fast you consume the budget relative to plan (burn rate 1 uses exactly the budget over the window). Google's SRE workbook recommends multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts, for example page if burn rate exceeds 14.4 over both 1 hour and 5 minutes (2 percent of a 30-day budget gone in an hour), and ticket for slower burns like 6 over 6 hours or 1 over 3 days. This catches fast outages quickly and slow leaks without noise.

How it works

  1. Pick user journeys and define SLIs as good/valid events.
  2. Set SLOs from user needs and historical performance.
  3. Compute error budgets and agree an error budget policy.
  4. Implement multi-window burn-rate alerts.
  5. Review budget status in release planning.
  6. Revisit SLOs quarterly.

Example

# 99.9% availability SLO, page on fast burn (14.4x over 1h and 5m)
- alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
  expr: |
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[1h])) / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
    and
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[5m])) / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[5m]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
  labels: { severity: page }

Common mistakes

  • SLIs on infrastructure metrics instead of user experience.
  • SLO equal to the SLA, leaving no safety margin.
  • 100 percent or "five nines" targets without justification.
  • An error budget with no policy, so nothing changes when it's spent.

How to answer in the interview

"An SLI is a measured ratio of good events, like the percentage of requests served successfully under 300 ms. An SLO is the internal target, say 99.9 percent over 30 days. An SLA is a customer contract with penalties and should be looser than the SLO. The error budget is 100 percent minus the SLO, so 0.1 percent, about 43 minutes a month. While budget remains we ship quickly; when it's exhausted we freeze risky releases and prioritise reliability, as agreed in an error budget policy. Alerts use multi-window burn rates rather than raw thresholds."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you choose an SLO for a new service? Start from user expectations and dependencies' SLOs, measure for a few weeks, set slightly below observed performance and tighten later.
  • What is burn rate? Budget consumption speed relative to an even spend over the window.
  • Should dependencies' SLOs affect yours? Yes; you can't exceed the reliability of hard dependencies without redundancy.
