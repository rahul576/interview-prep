What are scaling laws in AI, and what do they imply?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Scaling laws are empirical findings that model loss falls predictably as a power law with more compute, data and parameters; they guide compute-optimal model and data sizes (Chinchilla), imply diminishing returns and data limits, and now extend to test-time compute for reasoning.

Detailed explanation

Around 2020, researchers showed that language model loss decreases smoothly, roughly following a power law, as you increase model size, dataset size and training compute, provided none is bottlenecked. On a log-log plot the trend is nearly a straight line across many orders of magnitude. That made it possible to train small models, fit the curve and predict the loss of a much larger run before spending tens or hundreds of millions on it, which is how labs plan training runs and justify investment.

The Chinchilla work (2022) refined this: for a fixed compute budget there is a compute-optimal balance between parameters and training tokens, roughly scaling both together, about 20 tokens per parameter in that study. It showed many earlier models were undertrained for their size. Since then, models are often trained on far more tokens than compute-optimal, because a smaller model that is "over-trained" is cheaper to serve, shifting the optimum when inference cost counts.

Implications: capabilities have improved steadily with scale, but each fixed gain in loss needs a multiplicative increase in resources, so returns diminish and costs escalate. High-quality data is becoming a constraint, motivating better curation, synthetic data, multimodal data and RL-generated experience. Lower loss also does not map neatly to specific capabilities, which can appear abruptly on some benchmarks. A newer axis is test-time compute scaling: reasoning models that generate longer chains of thought, sample multiple attempts or search and verify improve on hard maths and coding as they are allowed to "think" more, trading inference cost and latency for quality.

How it works

  1. Train a family of smaller models across sizes and data amounts.
  2. Measure validation loss for each.
  3. Fit power-law curves relating loss to compute, parameters and tokens.
  4. Extrapolate to choose the size and token count for a big budget.
  5. Adjust for inference cost and data availability; for deployment, also scale reasoning effort at test time.

Example

Loss(N, D) ≈ E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta
  N = parameters, D = training tokens, E = irreducible loss

Compute-optimal (Chinchilla heuristic): D ≈ 20 × N
  70B parameters -> about 1.4 trillion tokens

Test-time scaling: same model, "reasoning effort" low / medium / high
  -> higher accuracy on hard tasks, more output tokens, more latency and cost

Common mistakes

  • Treating scaling laws as physical laws rather than empirical trends.
  • Assuming bigger is always the best business choice despite serving cost.
  • Ignoring data quality and quantity limits.
  • Equating lower perplexity with guaranteed new capabilities.

How to answer in the interview

"Scaling laws are the observation that model loss falls predictably, roughly as a power law, as you add compute, data and parameters. That lets labs forecast a big model's performance from small runs. Chinchilla showed there's a compute-optimal balance of model size and tokens, and that earlier models were undertrained, so the field moved to smaller models trained on more data, especially since those are cheaper to serve. The implications are diminishing returns, rising costs and data becoming a bottleneck. The newer axis is test-time compute, where letting models reason longer improves hard tasks at higher inference cost."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What does test-time scaling mean for application cost? Higher reasoning effort means more tokens and latency, so route only hard requests to it.
  • Why over-train smaller models? Inference dominates lifetime cost, so a smaller model with more training is cheaper overall.
  • What happens when data runs out? Synthetic data, better filtering, multimodal sources and RL environments.
