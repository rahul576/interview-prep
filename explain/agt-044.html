How does anomaly detection on operational metrics work, and how do you keep false positives low?

AI Agents & AIOps · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Anomaly detection learns a baseline of normal behaviour per metric, including seasonality, and flags significant deviations; keep false positives low by alerting on sustained, user-impacting deviations, combining signals, tuning per metric and using responder feedback.

Detailed explanation

A static threshold like "CPU above 80%" is wrong half the time: it fires during a normal Monday peak and stays silent when traffic drops to zero at 3 p.m. because something upstream broke. Anomaly detection instead models what the metric usually does at this time and alerts when reality departs from that expectation.

Most operational metrics have daily and weekly seasonality, trends and holiday effects, so the model must include them. Methods range from rolling mean and standard deviation bands, through seasonal decomposition (STL), Holt-Winters and other forecasting models, to multivariate ML models such as isolation forests or autoencoders across many metrics.

The hard part is false positives. With thousands of metrics, even a 1% false alarm rate means constant noise. Practical rules: require sustained deviation (for example 10 of the last 15 minutes) rather than single points; combine signals, such as a latency anomaly together with rising errors; tune sensitivity per metric; annotate known events like deployments and campaigns; and page only on user-impacting signals such as SLO burn rates, keeping internal metric anomalies as context. Finally, collect feedback from responders on whether alerts were useful and retune.

How it works

  1. Collect history (several weeks) per metric and series.
  2. Fit a model with daily/weekly seasonality and trend.
  3. Compute an expected value and a confidence band for each time step.
  4. Score deviations; require persistence and minimum absolute change.
  5. Correlate with other signals and suppress during annotated events.
  6. Route only user-impacting anomalies to paging; record feedback.

Example

A Prometheus-style seasonal band comparing now with the same time last week:

- record: job:http_requests:rate5m
  expr: sum by (job) (rate(http_requests_total[5m]))
- alert: TrafficDropAnomaly
  expr: |
    job:http_requests:rate5m
      < 0.5 * avg_over_time(job:http_requests:rate5m[1h] offset 1w)
    and job:http_requests:rate5m offset 1w > 100
  for: 15m
  labels: { severity: page }

The for: 15m requires persistence, and the minimum-volume condition avoids alerts on near-zero traffic.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring seasonality, so every morning peak is "anomalous".
  • Paging on every anomaly in every internal metric.
  • Alerting on relative change for tiny values (2 to 4 errors is +100%).
  • Never retraining as traffic patterns shift.
  • Not annotating deployments, migrations and campaigns.

How to answer in the interview

"Instead of fixed thresholds, anomaly detection learns what normal looks like for each metric, including daily and weekly patterns and trends, using anything from rolling bands and seasonal decomposition to ML models. Keeping false positives low is the real work. I alert only on sustained deviations, combine signals such as latency plus errors, tune sensitivity per metric, annotate known events like deployments, and page only on user-impacting signals like SLO burn rate, while other anomalies become context. Responder feedback on each alert is used to tune the models."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why are thousands of metrics noisier than expected? Small false-positive rates multiply across many series.
  • Static thresholds still useful? Yes, for hard limits like disk full or error budgets.
  • Univariate or multivariate? Univariate is easier to explain; multivariate catches combined patterns.
