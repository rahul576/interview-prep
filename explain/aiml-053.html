What is chain-of-thought prompting, and how does it relate to reasoning models?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting asks a model to reason step by step before answering, improving multi-step tasks at the cost of more tokens and latency; reasoning models are trained (largely with RL) to do this internally, and in both cases the written reasoning may not be faithful and should be separated from the user-facing answer.

Detailed explanation

A language model produces each token conditioned on the previous ones. If it must output the final answer immediately, all the work has to happen within a single forward pass per token. Asking it to write intermediate steps first gives it room to break the problem down, and each written step conditions the next, which improves accuracy on arithmetic word problems, logic, planning, code analysis and complex instructions with many constraints. Techniques include adding "think through this step by step", few-shot examples with worked solutions, asking for reasoning inside a dedicated section (for example <thinking> tags) followed by an <answer>, and self-consistency (sample several reasoning paths and take the majority answer).

The cost is more output tokens, so higher latency and price, and for simple tasks (lookup, short classification, formatting) it adds little or can even hurt.

Reasoning models build this in. They are trained, largely with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks, to generate an extended reasoning process (sometimes called thinking) before answering, including trying approaches, checking work and backtracking. They usually expose an effort level or thinking budget. With them, explicit "think step by step" prompts matter much less; instead you give clear goals and context and choose the effort level. Some APIs return the reasoning, a summary of it, or keep it hidden.

Two cautions. Faithfulness: the written reasoning is not always an accurate account of how the model actually reached its answer; studies show models sometimes omit factors that influenced them. Treat it as helpful for debugging, not as proof. Presentation: applications typically hide or separate the reasoning and show users a concise final answer, and parse only the answer section.

How it works

  1. Decide whether the task is multi-step enough to benefit.
  2. Prompt for reasoning in a separate section, or enable a reasoning model's thinking with a suitable effort.
  3. The model writes intermediate steps, then the final answer.
  4. The application extracts the answer section and discards or logs the reasoning.
  5. Evaluate accuracy versus latency and cost to pick the effort level.

Example

System: Solve the problem. First reason inside <thinking> tags,
then give only the final result inside <answer> tags.

User: A plan costs 1,200 a year with 15% off for annual billing, or 110 a month.
Which is cheaper over a year, and by how much?

Model: <thinking>Annual: 1,200 × 0.85 = 1,020. Monthly: 110 × 12 = 1,320.
Difference: 300.</thinking>
<answer>Annual billing is cheaper by 300 per year (1,020 vs 1,320).</answer>

Common mistakes

  • Using CoT on simple tasks and paying extra latency for nothing.
  • Showing raw reasoning to end users.
  • Treating reasoning text as a faithful audit trail.
  • Adding heavy step-by-step scaffolding to reasoning models that already think.

How to answer in the interview

"Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to work through a problem step by step before answering, which helps on multi-step tasks like maths, logic and planning, because each step conditions the next. The cost is more tokens and latency. Reasoning models are trained, mostly with reinforcement learning, to do this internally, often with an effort setting, so explicit CoT prompts matter less. Two cautions: the reasoning text isn't always faithful to how the model really got the answer, and I separate it from the concise final answer users see."

Likely follow-up questions

  • When avoid step-by-step reasoning? Simple lookups, classification or formatting, and latency-critical paths.
  • What is self-consistency? Sampling multiple reasoning paths and taking the majority answer.
  • How pick reasoning effort? Evaluate accuracy, latency and cost at each level on your own tasks.
