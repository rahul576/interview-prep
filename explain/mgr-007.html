A critical project is at risk of missing a committed customer date. How do you manage it?

Delivery & Execution · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Assess reality with the team (remaining work, critical path, risks), escalate early with no surprises, offer options (descope, focus, parallelise, add specific expertise, renegotiate), protect the team from sustained overtime, and keep frequent, transparent tracking until delivery.

Detailed explanation

The interviewer is testing judgement and integrity under pressure. Weak answers promise heroics or keep quiet hoping to catch up. Strong answers face facts quickly, involve stakeholders, and choose deliberately among trade-offs.

Assess reality: sit with the team and re-estimate the remaining work honestly, bottom-up, identifying the critical path and the top risks (an unfinished integration, a vendor dependency, performance testing). Compare with historical throughput, not hope. Ask "what would have to be true to hit the date?"

Escalate early: bad news ages badly. Tell your manager and the account or product owner as soon as the risk is real, with facts and options rather than just a problem. "No surprises" builds trust even when the news is bad.

Options: descope to what the customer really needs on day one (often a smaller first release is acceptable), remove distractions (pause other projects, protect from support), parallelise work or bring in specific expertise for a bottleneck (adding random people late slows things down, per Brooks's law), simplify the technical approach, or renegotiate the date with a credible plan. Often a combination works: a phased delivery with core scope on the date.

Sustainability: a short, voluntary push can be fine, but sustained overtime increases defects and attrition. Tracking: daily check-ins on the critical path, a simple burn-up, and a regular status update to stakeholders with honest RAG status.

How it works

  1. Re-plan bottom-up with the team; identify the critical path.
  2. Quantify the gap and risk.
  3. Prepare two or three options with impact.
  4. Escalate to stakeholders and agree a decision.
  5. Execute: focus, remove blockers daily.
  6. Report progress frequently; re-plan if reality changes.

Example

Situation: Six weeks before a committed go-live for a logistics customer, our burn-up showed we would finish about three weeks late, mainly because the carrier integration was harder than expected. Task: As engineering lead, I had to protect the customer relationship and the team. Action: We re-estimated with the team and found the critical path ran through the carrier integration and load testing. I told my director and the account manager that week, with three options: delay three weeks, launch on time with two carriers instead of five, or add a contractor who knew that carrier API. We met the customer, who said two carriers covered 80% of their volume. We paused a non-urgent internal project to give the integration two more engineers, and I sent a short weekly status with a burn-up chart. Result: We went live on the date with two carriers and added the rest within four weeks. The customer renewed, and nobody worked a weekend.

Common mistakes

  • Hiding the risk until the last week.
  • Promising the date and relying on overtime.
  • Adding many new people late in the project.
  • Escalating problems without options.
  • No follow-up tracking after the decision.

How to answer in the interview

"First I'd assess reality with the team: the remaining work, the critical path and the biggest risks, without optimism. Then I'd escalate early to stakeholders, because bad news gets worse with time. I'd come with options: descope to what the customer needs first, remove distractions so the team can focus, add specific expertise to the bottleneck, or renegotiate the date with a clear plan. A short push can be fine, but I'd protect the team from sustained overtime. Then I'd give frequent, transparent updates against milestones."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What would you do differently next time? Earlier risk spikes, ranges instead of fixed dates, milestone checkpoints.
  • Customer refuses any change? Clarify which constraint is real, escalate jointly, offer interim workarounds.
  • Why not add more developers? Onboarding and coordination costs; only targeted expertise helps late.
