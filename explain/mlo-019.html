How do you decide how often to retrain a model?

MLOps · concept · levels mid, senior, lead

In one line

Base retraining cadence on evidence: the measured degradation rate, label lag, the cost of retraining versus the cost of a stale model, and backtests of different cadences; use scheduled or triggered retraining as fits, and put automated validation gates on every retrain.

Detailed explanation

"Retrain weekly" is a guess unless you know how fast the model decays. I measure decay by evaluating older model versions on newer data: train on January, test on February, March, April, and plot how performance falls. A demand model may be stable for months; a click-through model may be stale in a day.

Label lag caps freshness. If fraud labels need 45 days to mature, the newest usable training data is 45 days old, so retraining daily mainly adds cost. Costs include compute (GPU hours), pipeline maintenance and human review effort, balanced against the business cost of a stale model: lost revenue, missed fraud, poor recommendations.

Backtesting turns this into a decision: simulate monthly, weekly and daily retraining on history and compare the gains. Often the curve flattens quickly, showing a sensible cadence.

There are two styles. Scheduled retraining suits predictable cycles, for example a demand forecast retrained before each month. Triggered retraining fires on significant drift, performance below threshold or a known business change such as a new product line. Many teams combine both: a regular schedule plus triggers.

Whatever the cadence, each retrain passes automated gates against the current production model. Fully automatic retraining on bad data is one of the most common ways to ship a worse model.

How it works

  1. Measure decay by scoring past models on later periods.
  2. Note label maturity time and data availability.
  3. Backtest candidate cadences and compare metric gains to cost.
  4. Choose schedule, triggers or both.
  5. Gate every retrain: data validation, champion comparison, segment checks.
  6. Revisit cadence when the business or data changes.

Example

Backtest (churn model, AUC on following month)
  Retrain quarterly : 0.781
  Retrain monthly   : 0.794
  Retrain weekly    : 0.796   (+4x compute, +review effort)
Decision: monthly schedule + trigger if AUC on matured labels < 0.77
          or PSI > 0.25 on any top-5 feature.

Common mistakes

  • Picking a cadence by habit rather than measurement.
  • Retraining more often than labels allow.
  • Auto-promoting without comparing with production.
  • Ignoring the human cost of reviewing each new model.

How to answer in the interview

"I don't pick 'weekly' upfront. I measure how fast performance degrades by testing older models on newer data, check label lag, which limits how fresh training data can be, and weigh retraining cost against the cost of a stale model. Backtesting different cadences on history usually shows where gains flatten. Some models suit a schedule, others triggers like drift or a performance drop, often both. And every retrain passes automated gates against the production model, because automatic retraining on bad data is a common way to ship a worse model."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What can go wrong with fully automated retraining and promotion? Training on broken or poisoned data, silent regressions on segments, and feedback loops.
  • How do you handle label lag? Train on matured labels only and use proxies for early monitoring.
  • Is online learning an alternative? Yes for very fast-changing problems, with stronger safeguards.
