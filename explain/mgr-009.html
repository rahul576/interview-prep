How do you communicate a major production incident to executives and customers?

Stakeholder Management · managerial · levels senior, lead

In one line

Communicate early, honestly and regularly, through one communication owner and a status page, in plain business language: what we know, what we are doing, and when the next update comes; follow with a postmortem summary and actions.

Detailed explanation

During a major incident, poor communication can damage trust more than the outage itself. Executives and customers mostly want to know: Is it affecting me? How bad is it? Are you on it? When will I hear more? A good process answers those consistently.

Separate roles. In a typical incident command structure there is an incident commander coordinating the response, technical responders fixing the issue, and a communications lead who writes and sends updates. This keeps engineers focused and messaging consistent.

Communicate early: the first acknowledgement should go out within minutes of confirming customer impact, even before the cause is known. Be honest without speculating. Use a regular cadence, such as every 30 minutes for a major incident, and always state the next update time, even if there is no news.

Tailor the message. Executives need business impact: which customers, regions or features are affected, revenue and SLA exposure, and whether any regulatory or contractual notification is needed. Customers need clear, non-technical language on the status page, and enterprise accounts may need direct contact from account managers. Avoid blame, internal jargon and unconfirmed root causes.

Afterwards, publish a blameless postmortem internally and a summary for customers and executives: timeline, impact, root cause, and action items with owners and dates. Follow through on those actions, because broken promises hurt trust next time.

How it works

  1. Declare the incident and assign incident commander and communications lead.
  2. Send the first acknowledgement quickly: impact known so far, investigating, next update time.
  3. Update on a fixed cadence through the status page and an executive channel.
  4. Announce mitigation and monitoring, then resolution.
  5. Share a postmortem summary with actions, owners and dates.
  6. Track actions to completion.

Example

A first external status page update:

[Investigating] 10:42 UTC – Some customers in Europe are unable to complete
card payments. Other payment methods are working. Our engineers are actively
investigating. Next update by 11:15 UTC.

Executive channel update: "Impact: ~18% of EU card payments failing since 10:31, approx. £40k/hour in delayed revenue. Mitigation in progress: failing over to secondary processor. Next update 11:15."

Common mistakes

  • Waiting for the root cause before saying anything.
  • Engineers fixing the issue also writing all updates.
  • Technical jargon or speculation in customer messages.
  • Missing an announced update time.
  • Blaming a vendor or individual publicly.

How to answer in the interview

"I start communicating early, as soon as we confirm customer impact, even before we know the cause. One person owns communication, separate from the engineers fixing the issue, and updates go out on a fixed cadence, for example every 30 minutes, through the status page and an executive channel. For executives I focus on business impact in plain language: which customers and features are affected, and the revenue or SLA risk. Every update says what we know, what we are doing, and when the next update will come. Afterwards I share a blameless postmortem summary with the root cause and action items with owners and dates, and make sure those actions are completed."

Likely follow-up questions

  • What should never go into the first external update? Speculation about root cause, blame, internal names or estimates you cannot keep.
  • How do you handle a customer demanding a root cause during the incident? Acknowledge, share what is known, and commit to the postmortem.
  • When do you involve legal or security? When data exposure, regulatory reporting or contractual SLAs are involved.
