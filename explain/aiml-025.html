What is transfer learning, and why is it so important in practice?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

Transfer learning reuses a model pretrained on large, general data and fine-tunes it (or trains a small head on it) for a new task, needing far less data and compute; foundation models are the extreme form, limited mainly by domain mismatch.

Detailed explanation

Training a deep network from scratch needs huge labelled datasets and lots of compute. But the early and middle layers of a model trained on broad data learn general features: edges, textures and shapes for images; syntax, semantics and world knowledge for text. Those features transfer to many related tasks. So instead of starting from random weights, we start from pretrained weights and adapt.

There are several levels. Feature extraction: freeze the backbone and train only a new head (for example a classifier layer); cheap and good with very little data. Partial fine-tuning: unfreeze the top layers, often with a lower learning rate. Full fine-tuning: update all weights; best when you have more data and the domain differs. For large language models, parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA trains small adapter matrices while the base weights stay frozen, cutting memory and storage drastically.

Classic examples: an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet fine-tuned on a few thousand photos to spot product defects; BERT-style encoders fine-tuned for sentiment, intent or entity recognition; speech models adapted to an accent or domain vocabulary. Foundation models push this further: one huge pretrained model adapted to countless tasks through fine-tuning or simply prompting (in-context learning), with no weight changes at all.

Benefits: much less labelled data, faster training, lower cost and usually better accuracy than training from scratch. Limits: gains shrink when the target domain is very different (satellite or medical imagery versus everyday photos, legal jargon versus web text), aggressive fine-tuning on small data can cause catastrophic forgetting or overfitting, and you inherit the base model's biases and licence terms.

How it works

  1. Choose a pretrained model whose pretraining data is close to your domain.
  2. Replace the output head to match your task.
  3. Freeze most layers and train the head on your data.
  4. Optionally unfreeze more layers with a small learning rate.
  5. Validate on held-out data and compare with a from-scratch or simpler baseline.

Example

import torch.nn as nn
from torchvision.models import resnet50, ResNet50_Weights

model = resnet50(weights=ResNet50_Weights.DEFAULT)
for p in model.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False                    # freeze backbone
model.fc = nn.Linear(model.fc.in_features, 2)  # defect / ok head
# later: unfreeze model.layer4 and fine-tune with lr=1e-4

Common mistakes

  • Using a high learning rate that destroys pretrained features.
  • Different input preprocessing than the pretrained model expects.
  • Fine-tuning everything on tiny data and overfitting.
  • Ignoring licence and bias of the base model.

How to answer in the interview

"Transfer learning means starting from a model pretrained on large, general data and adapting it, rather than training from scratch. Because the model already knows general features, I can freeze most of it and train a small head, or fine-tune some or all layers with a low learning rate, using a fraction of the data and compute. Typical examples are ImageNet models for defect detection and BERT for text classification. Foundation models take this to the extreme, adapted by fine-tuning, LoRA or just prompting. The main limit is when my domain is very different from the pretraining data."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Freeze most layers or fine-tune everything? Freeze with little data and a similar domain; fine-tune more with more data or a different domain.
  • What is catastrophic forgetting? Losing general capabilities when fine-tuning narrowly.
  • What is LoRA? Training small low-rank update matrices while the base weights stay frozen.
