Describe the stages of the machine learning lifecycle in production.

MLOps · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

The production ML lifecycle runs from problem framing and success metrics, through data collection, labelling and preparation, training and experimentation, evaluation against baselines, to deployment and serving, then monitoring and retraining, which loops back, making it a cycle.

Detailed explanation

Problem framing decides whether ML is even appropriate. What decision will the model support, what happens when it is wrong, and which metrics matter? Define both model metrics (precision, recall, RMSE) and business metrics (fraud losses avoided, conversion), plus constraints such as latency, explainability and regulation. A simple rule-based baseline is set here.

Data collection, labelling and preparation usually consumes most of the effort: finding sources, getting access, cleaning, joining, handling missing values, labelling (with quality checks), engineering features, and splitting data into training, validation and test sets, often split by time to avoid leakage.

Training and experimentation tries algorithms, features and hyperparameters, with every run tracked so results are comparable and reproducible.

Evaluation and validation compares candidates against the baseline and the current production model, on overall metrics and on important segments (regions, customer types), with checks for fairness, robustness, latency and model size.

Deployment and serving packages the model and its preprocessing and serves it: real-time behind an API, batch scoring jobs, streaming, or on devices. Rollouts are gradual: shadow mode, canary or A/B tests.

Monitoring watches data quality, drift, prediction distributions and, once labels arrive, real performance and business KPIs. Findings feed a retraining loop.

How it works

  1. Frame the problem, metrics and baseline.
  2. Collect, label and prepare data; build features.
  3. Train and track experiments.
  4. Evaluate against baseline, production model and segments.
  5. Deploy gradually in the right serving mode.
  6. Monitor and trigger retraining or redesign.

Example

A bank's card-fraud model: success means catching more fraud at a fixed false-positive rate, with p99 latency under 50 ms. Data is six months of transactions labelled by chargebacks, split by time. Gradient-boosted trees beat the rules baseline by 18% recall. The model runs in shadow for two weeks, then serves 10% of traffic.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping problem framing and optimising a metric nobody cares about.
  • Random splits on time-dependent data, causing leakage.
  • Evaluating only aggregate metrics.
  • Treating deployment as the end of the project.

How to answer in the interview

"It starts with problem framing: which decision the model supports and which metrics matter, both model metrics like precision and business metrics like reduced fraud losses. Then data collection, labelling and preparation, including features and train, validation and test splits. Training and experimentation follow, with every run tracked. Evaluation compares candidates with a baseline and the current production model, including segments, fairness and latency. Deployment serves the model in real time, batch or on devices, usually gradually. Finally monitoring watches data quality, drift and outcomes and feeds a retraining loop, so it's a cycle."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which stage do teams most often underinvest in? Monitoring and the retraining loop, followed closely by data quality and labelling.
  • What is shadow deployment? The model scores live traffic without affecting decisions, so you can compare it safely.
  • Why split data by time? To mimic production, where you always predict the future from the past.
