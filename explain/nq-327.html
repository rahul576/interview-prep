What happens during daily stand-ups and sprint retrospectives?

Agile & Scrum · concept · levels entry

In one line

The daily stand-up is a 15-minute sync for the developers to inspect progress towards the sprint goal and surface blockers; the sprint retrospective is the end-of-sprint meeting where the team inspects how it works and agrees a few concrete, owned improvement actions.

Detailed explanation

In Scrum the stand-up is formally the Daily Scrum. The 2020 Scrum Guide does not prescribe the classic three questions (what I did yesterday, what I will do today, any blockers); it only says the developers inspect progress towards the sprint goal and adapt the plan. Many teams still use the three questions, while mature teams prefer walking the board from right to left, focusing on the items closest to done, which keeps attention on finishing work rather than on individual activity. It is not a status report to a manager: the scrum master facilitates and helps remove impediments, but the meeting belongs to the developers. Detailed discussions are taken offline straight after, in a "parking lot", with only the people who need to be there.

The retrospective closes the sprint, after the sprint review. The team looks at people, interactions, processes, tools and the definition of done, asking what went well, what did not and what to try next. Formats such as start / stop / continue, mad / sad / glad, sailboat or 4Ls keep it fresh. The output is not a long list of complaints but one to three concrete actions, each with an owner, ideally added to the next sprint backlog.

Both ceremonies depend on psychological safety. A retro is blameless: it discusses systems and decisions, not people. And it only builds trust if last time's actions are reviewed at the start of the next retro.

How it works

  1. Stand-up at the same time and place daily, time-boxed to 15 minutes, with the board on screen.
  2. Walk the board right to left, or each person answers the three questions briefly, always linked to the sprint goal.
  3. Blockers are called out and flagged; the scrum master or a teammate picks up follow-up.
  4. Long discussions go to a parking lot after the stand-up.
  5. Retro: review previous actions, gather data, generate insights, decide one to three actions, close.
  6. Actions get owners and go into the sprint backlog so they are visible and tracked.

Example

A retro board after a sprint where a release slipped:

Previous actions: "Add contract tests for payments API" -> done (owner: Priya)
Went well:   pairing on the search feature; fast code reviews
Didn't:      staging broken for 2 days; 3 stories blocked waiting on API team
Try:         WIP limit of 2 per person in "In Review"
Actions:
  1. Add a staging health check alert to Slack        owner: Ravi    due: next sprint
  2. Weekly 15-min sync with the API team             owner: Anna    due: from Monday

Common mistakes

  • Turning the stand-up into a 40-minute status report or problem-solving session.
  • Reporting to the scrum master or manager instead of coordinating with each other.
  • Retros that generate many ideas but no owned actions, or actions that are never revisited.
  • Blaming individuals, which kills honesty in future retros.
  • Skipping the retro "because we are busy", exactly when it is most needed.

How to answer in the interview

"The daily stand-up is a fifteen-minute sync for the developers, focused on the sprint goal. We usually walk the board from right to left, talking about what moved, what is next and what is blocked. It is not a status report to a manager, and any detailed discussion happens straight afterwards with only the people involved. The scrum master helps remove the impediments we raise. The retrospective happens at the end of every sprint. We first check last sprint's actions, then use a format like start, stop, continue to discuss what went well and what did not, and we agree one to three concrete actions with owners, for example adding contract tests or a WIP limit. It only works if it is blameless and the actions are actually followed up."

Likely follow-up questions

  • How do you make sure retro actions are actually done? Give each an owner and due date, add it to the sprint backlog, and review it at the start of the next retro.
  • What if the stand-up regularly overruns? Enforce the time-box, use a parking lot, and walk the board instead of round-robin updates.
  • Who attends the stand-up? The developers; the scrum master facilitates, and the product owner may attend as a listener or participant.
