AI alert correlation merged a new outage into an existing low-priority incident, delaying response by an hour. What do you change?

AI Agents & AIOps · scenario · levels mid, senior

In one line

Over-grouping hid a new outage, so make high-severity and SLO-based alerts page independently, raise incident priority when a more severe alert joins, tighten correlation rules and windows, highlight new symptoms in grouped incidents, review correlation in postmortems, and measure missed or delayed incidents, not just noise reduction.

Detailed explanation

The correlation engine did what it was optimised for: reducing noise. It saw alerts close in time on related services and attached them to an existing low-priority incident. Because the incident was already acknowledged and low priority, nobody was paged for the new, more severe failure, and response was delayed by an hour.

The fix is to change both the rules and what is measured. First, severity must win over grouping: SLO burn-rate alerts on user-facing services, or any P1-class signal, always page, even if correlation also attaches them to an incident. Attaching a higher-severity alert to an incident should automatically escalate the incident's priority and notify the incident commander. Second, tune correlation: shorter time windows, and require stronger evidence, such as direct topology dependency or high content similarity, before merging. Long-running incidents should not act as magnets that absorb everything nearby. Third, the incident view must highlight new symptoms added after creation, with a notification to responders.

Finally, make correlation part of the postmortem review and track the right metrics: missed or delayed incidents caused by grouping, the split rate (how often responders split merged incidents) and time to page for high-severity alerts.

How it works

  1. Classify alerts by severity and user impact before correlation.
  2. High severity: page immediately, then correlate for context.
  3. Merging requires topology or similarity above a threshold, inside a short window.
  4. Attaching a higher-severity alert raises incident priority and notifies.
  5. New alert types in an incident are highlighted.
  6. Postmortems review grouping decisions; metrics tracked weekly.

Example

correlation_policy:
  max_window: 10m
  merge_requires: [topology_adjacent, or: text_similarity >= 0.8]
  never_suppress_paging_for:
    - severity: critical
    - alertname: SLOBurnRateFast
  on_attach_higher_severity:
    escalate_incident_priority: true
    notify: incident_commander
  incident_age_limit_for_merge: 2h
metrics: [delayed_pages_due_to_grouping, incident_split_rate, time_to_page_p1]

Common mistakes

  • Judging correlation only by percentage of alerts suppressed.
  • Letting old, open incidents absorb new alerts indefinitely.
  • Severity of an incident fixed at creation.
  • No feedback mechanism for responders to split incidents.

How to answer in the interview

"The correlation optimised for noise and merged a new severe outage into a low-priority incident, so nobody was paged. I'd make high-severity and SLO burn-rate alerts always page independently, even if they're attached for context, and have a higher-severity alert automatically escalate the incident. I'd tighten rules: shorter windows, stronger topology or similarity evidence, and no merging into old incidents. New symptoms in a grouped incident should be highlighted. And I'd review correlation in postmortems and measure delayed or missed incidents and split rate, not just noise reduction."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Metric to catch this early? Time to page for high-severity alerts and split rate.
  • Disable correlation? No, keep it but subordinate it to severity.
  • Who reviews rules? SRE team, with changes tested on replayed alerts.
