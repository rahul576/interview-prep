What are open-weight and closed models, and how do you think about the trade-offs?

AI/ML & GenAI · concept · levels mid, senior

In one line

Closed models are used only via APIs and usually lead on the hardest tasks with managed infrastructure; open-weight models can be downloaded, self-hosted and fine-tuned, giving control, privacy and cost flexibility at the price of operational effort, with licences that must be checked.

Detailed explanation

Closed models, such as the flagship Claude, GPT and Gemini models, are accessed through the provider's API or through cloud platforms (for example Amazon Bedrock, Google Vertex AI or Azure). You never get the weights. Benefits: typically the strongest capability on complex reasoning, coding and agentic work, large context windows, multimodality, built-in safety systems, scaling and support, and you pay per token with no GPUs to manage. Downsides: data leaves your environment (mitigated by enterprise terms, regional processing and no-training commitments), you depend on the vendor's pricing, rate limits and model deprecation schedule, and customisation is limited to prompting and whatever fine-tuning the vendor offers.

Open-weight models, such as the Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek and Gemma families, publish weights. You can run them on-premises, in your VPC or even air-gapped, pin exact versions forever, fine-tune them fully or with LoRA, quantise them and control latency. For high-volume narrow tasks (classification, extraction, routing), a small fine-tuned open model can be much cheaper than an API. "Open-weight" is not always "open source": training data and code are often unpublished, and licences range from permissive Apache 2.0 or MIT to custom terms with usage restrictions or user-count thresholds.

The capability gap between the best open and closed models has narrowed substantially, but frontier closed models usually still lead on the hardest tasks. Self-hosting brings real costs: GPU capacity, an inference stack (vLLM, TGI, TensorRT-LLM), autoscaling, monitoring, security patching and your own safety guardrails. Many organisations use both, routing by use case: a closed frontier model for complex customer-facing reasoning, and an open model for sensitive internal data or cheap bulk processing, behind a common gateway.

How it works

  1. Define requirements: quality bar, data sensitivity, latency, volume, budget.
  2. Shortlist closed and open candidates.
  3. Run the same internal evaluation on each.
  4. Estimate total cost: tokens for APIs; GPUs, engineering and operations for self-hosting.
  5. Check licences, data-processing terms and deprecation policies; choose per use case behind a gateway.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming open-weight is free; GPUs and operations cost real money.
  • Calling every open-weight model "open source" without reading the licence.
  • Picking one approach for all use cases.
  • Ignoring vendor model deprecation timelines in planning.

How to answer in the interview

"Closed models like Claude, GPT and Gemini are only available through APIs; they usually lead on hard reasoning and coding and come fully managed. Open-weight models like Llama, Qwen, Mistral or DeepSeek can be downloaded, self-hosted and fine-tuned, which gives control over data, versions and cost, useful for privacy, air-gapped setups or high-volume narrow tasks. The trade-off is GPU infrastructure and ops expertise, and licences vary, so I check them. In practice I'd evaluate both on our tasks and often use both, routing per use case."

Likely follow-up questions

  • When choose open-weight for a customer-facing feature? Strict data residency, very high volume at low cost, or a narrow task where a fine-tuned small model matches quality.
  • What is a model gateway? A layer that routes requests across providers with auth, logging, cost tracking and fallback.
  • How do you handle API model deprecation? Pin versions, track deprecation notices and re-run evals before migrating.
