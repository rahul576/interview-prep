Shadow, canary or straight cutover: which would you pick for a new fraud model, and why?

MLOps · scenario · levels senior, lead

In one line

For a new fraud model I usually choose shadow first, then a canary, because label lag (chargebacks arrive weeks later) prevents quick accuracy checks; shadow is safe but cannot show real downstream effects, a canary shows them but risks a slice, and straight cutover needs instant rollback and strong offline evidence.

Detailed explanation

The key constraint in fraud is label lag: true outcomes such as chargebacks or confirmed fraud reports arrive days to weeks after a transaction. You cannot release a model on Monday and know by Wednesday whether it catches more fraud. So rollout decisions rely on proxies and safety.

Shadow mode runs the new model on live traffic and logs its scores and would-be decisions without acting. It is risk-free for customers and reveals score distributions, decline and review rates, latency, error rates and agreement with the current model. Disagreements can be sampled for analyst review. But shadow cannot reveal feedback effects: how fraudsters adapt, how customers react to declines, or how the review team copes.

A canary lets the new model actually decide for a small slice (say 5 percent of traffic, ideally a lower-risk segment). It shows real effects, but if the model is bad that slice suffers more fraud loss or more false declines. Guardrail metrics and automatic rollback limit the damage.

A straight cutover is acceptable only with strong offline backtesting, a tested one-step rollback, and low stakes; for fraud it is rarely the right default. I'd also consider running the new model as a challenger permanently on a small slice, giving ongoing comparison once labels arrive.

How it works

  1. Backtest offline on recent labelled data, including recent fraud patterns.
  2. Shadow for one to two weeks: compare distributions, decline rates, latency, disagreements.
  3. Review a sample of disagreements with fraud analysts.
  4. Canary at 1 to 5 percent on lower-risk segments with automatic guardrails.
  5. Ramp gradually while early labels and proxies stay healthy.
  6. Keep the old model ready for one-step rollback until labels confirm.

Example

canary:
  traffic_percent: 5
  segments: [domestic, amount_lt_200]
  guardrails:              # any breach -> automatic rollback
    decline_rate_delta_max: 0.3pp
    manual_review_rate_max: 1.5%
    p99_latency_ms_max: 80
    customer_complaints_per_10k_max: 2

Common mistakes

  • Expecting to measure accuracy in days when labels take weeks.
  • Treating shadow agreement as proof the model is better.
  • Running a canary without predefined guardrails and automatic rollback.
  • Picking the highest-risk segment for the first canary.

How to answer in the interview

"There's no universal answer, so I'd start with label lag: fraud labels arrive weeks later, so we can't quickly prove accuracy in production. I'd run shadow mode first, scoring live traffic without acting, to check score distributions, decline rates, latency and disagreements with the current model. Then a canary on a small, lower-risk slice with guardrails like decline rate, review volume and latency that roll back automatically. A straight cutover would need strong backtesting and an instant rollback, which I'd rarely accept for fraud."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which guardrails stop the canary? Decline rate, manual review volume, latency, complaint rate and early fraud signals.
  • How long to shadow? Long enough to cover weekly cycles and peaks, usually one to two weeks.
  • How to confirm the win later? Compare fraud loss and false-positive rates per arm once labels mature.
