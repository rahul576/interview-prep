How do you govern hundreds of AWS accounts without slowing teams down?

Cloud (AWS) · design · levels lead

In one line

Govern at scale with a few strong preventive guardrails (SCPs, RCPs, declarative policies), broad detective controls (Config, Security Hub, cost anomaly detection) routed to owners, self-service account vending and golden templates, clear ownership and tagging, and a time-limited exceptions process reviewed continuously.

Detailed explanation

Governance fails in two ways: too loose, and you get breaches and bills; too strict, and teams wait weeks or work around controls. The goal is paved roads: the secure way is also the easiest way.

Preventive guardrails cover only things that must never happen, because they block without context: disabling CloudTrail, GuardDuty or Config; using unapproved regions; leaving the organisation; creating IAM users with access keys; disabling S3 Block Public Access; using the root user. SCPs cap principals, resource control policies cap access to resources (for example, deny access from outside the organisation), and declarative policies enforce service configuration such as blocking public AMI sharing or requiring IMDSv2. Test them in a staging OU and keep the list short.

Detective controls catch everything else: AWS Config rules and conformance packs, Security Hub standards, IAM Access Analyzer for external and unused access, Inspector, and Cost Anomaly Detection with budgets. Findings route automatically to the owning team's channel and ticket queue, with severity-based SLAs and, where safe, automated remediation.

Self-service: teams request accounts through a portal or pull request, and account vending (Control Tower Account Factory or AFT) delivers networking, logging, SSO, budgets and security services in minutes. Golden templates and modules (Service Catalog, Terraform modules, CDK constructs) make compliant architectures the default. Policy as code in CI (cfn-guard, Checkov, OPA) gives developers feedback before deployment.

Ownership: every account has a named owner, cost centre and data classification enforced by tag policies; scorecards per team show compliance and cost. Exceptions follow a documented process with risk acceptance, compensating controls and an expiry date. Review guardrail effectiveness with metrics: number of findings, time to remediate, blocked requests and developer feedback.

How it works

  1. Define a small set of non-negotiable preventive rules.
  2. Enable organisation-wide detective controls.
  3. Route findings to owners with SLAs and dashboards.
  4. Offer vending, templates and CI policy checks.
  5. Run an exceptions process with expiry.
  6. Review metrics quarterly and adjust.

Common mistakes

  • Dozens of SCPs that block legitimate work and drive shadow IT.
  • Findings sent to a central team nobody can act on.
  • Exceptions without expiry that become permanent.
  • No owner or tags, so nobody answers for cost or risk.

How to answer in the interview

"I combine a few strong preventive guardrails with broad detective controls and self-service. Preventive SCPs and RCPs cover things that must never happen, like disabling CloudTrail, unapproved regions, IAM users with keys or public buckets, kept few so they rarely block legitimate work. Config, Security Hub and cost anomaly detection catch the rest and alert the owning team. Teams get accounts via automated vending with networking, logging, SSO and budgets in minutes, plus golden IaC templates. Every account has an owner, tags and a budget, exceptions are time-limited and documented, and we review controls against metrics."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Which three SCPs first? Protect security services and logs, deny leaving the organisation, and restrict regions.
  • How measure whether governance slows teams? Lead time for new accounts, blocked deployments and developer surveys alongside risk metrics.
  • Who owns remediation? The workload team, with the platform team providing tooling and escalation.
