How does Azure RBAC work, and how is it different from Azure Policy?

Azure · concept · levels entry, mid

In one line

Azure RBAC decides who can do what, where: a role assignment binds a security principal to a role definition at a scope; Azure Policy decides what resources are allowed to look like, regardless of who deploys them, and reports compliance.

Detailed explanation

A role assignment has three parts. The security principal is a user, group, service principal or managed identity. The role definition is a set of allowed operations: Actions and NotActions for the control plane (managing resources) and DataActions for the data plane (for example reading blobs or Key Vault secrets). The scope is a management group, subscription, resource group or single resource, and assignments inherit down to child scopes. Permissions are additive: the effective access is the union of all assignments.

Key built-in roles are Owner (full access including assigning roles), Contributor (manage everything but not assign roles), Reader, and User Access Administrator, plus hundreds of service-specific roles such as Storage Blob Data Reader or AKS Cluster User. You can create custom roles when needed. Best practice is to assign roles to groups, not individuals, and follow least privilege.

Azure Policy works differently. A policy definition describes conditions on resource properties, with an effect: Deny (block the request), Audit, Modify or Append (change the request, such as adding tags), DeployIfNotExists (deploy a related resource, such as diagnostic settings), or AuditIfNotExists. Policies are grouped into initiatives and assigned at a scope with optional exemptions. The compliance dashboard shows non-compliant resources, and remediation tasks can fix existing ones.

They complement each other: RBAC says a Contributor can create storage accounts; Policy says those storage accounts must not allow public access. For administrative roles, Privileged Identity Management (PIM) provides just-in-time, time-limited, approved and audited elevation instead of standing access.

How it works

  1. A request reaches Azure Resource Manager with the caller's token.
  2. RBAC checks role assignments for that principal at the scope and parents.
  3. If allowed, Azure Policy evaluates the request against assigned policies.
  4. A Deny blocks it; Modify/Append alter it; Audit records non-compliance.
  5. After deployment, DeployIfNotExists and periodic compliance scans run.

Example

# RBAC: group gets Contributor on one resource group
az role assignment create --assignee-object-id <group-id> \
  --role Contributor --scope /subscriptions/<sub>/resourceGroups/rg-payments

# Policy: deny storage accounts that allow public blob access
az policy assignment create --name deny-public-blob \
  --policy "<built-in definition id for storage public access>" \
  --scope /subscriptions/<sub>

Common mistakes

  • Thinking Policy grants or restricts users; it evaluates resources.
  • Giving Owner or Contributor broadly "to unblock" people.
  • Assuming Contributor includes data-plane access such as reading blobs with Entra auth.
  • Assigning roles to individuals rather than groups.
  • Standing admin access instead of PIM.

How to answer in the interview

"Azure RBAC controls who can do what: a role assignment links a security principal, like a group or managed identity, to a role definition at a scope, from management group down to a single resource, and it inherits down. Built-in roles include Owner, Contributor and Reader, plus service-specific data-plane roles. Azure Policy is different: it controls what resources may look like regardless of who deploys them, for example denying public IPs or requiring tags, and reports compliance. I combine least-privilege RBAC assigned to groups, policy guardrails at management group level, and PIM for just-in-time admin access."

Likely follow-up questions

  • A Contributor can still create a public storage account. How do you prevent that? Assign a Deny policy for public network or blob access.
  • What is the difference between control plane and data plane roles? Managing the resource versus accessing its data.
  • What are deny assignments? Explicit denials created by Azure itself, for example by managed applications or deployment stacks.
