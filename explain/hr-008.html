Describe a significant technical decision you drove. How did you evaluate the options and get buy-in?

Leadership · behavioral · levels lead

In one line

Show structured decision-making and influence: frame the problem, evaluate options and trade-offs with evidence (data, proof of concept, benchmarks), build alignment through an RFC/ADR and review with affected teams, and report a measurable outcome, using STAR.

Detailed explanation

Leads are expected to make consequential technical decisions and bring people along. The interviewer evaluates how you think (problem first, options, criteria), how you use evidence rather than opinion, and how you influence without relying on authority.

Frame the problem: what business or engineering pain drove the decision (slow deployments, costs, outages, hiring)? Define decision criteria up front: cost, team skills, operational load, portability, security, time to deliver.

Options: always consider at least two or three, including "do nothing" or "improve the current approach". Compare them honestly; acknowledge weaknesses of the option you chose.

Evidence: a time-boxed proof of concept, benchmarks, cost models, reference calls with other companies, data from incidents. Evidence turns debates about preferences into discussions of facts.

Alignment: write an RFC (request for comments) describing context, options, recommendation and risks, and gather feedback from architects, security, operations and the teams affected. Incorporate concerns visibly; people support what they helped shape. Record the outcome in an ADR (architecture decision record) so future engineers know why. Plan the rollout incrementally with a reversal path.

Outcome: measurable results and a reflection, including what you would do differently. Being honest about a downside you underestimated shows maturity.

How it works

  1. Situation: the problem and why a decision was needed.
  2. Task: your role in driving it.
  3. Action: criteria, options, evidence, RFC, stakeholder engagement, rollout.
  4. Result: quantified impact.
  5. Reflection: lessons learned.

Example

Situation: Our 30 services ran on hand-maintained VMs with Ansible. Deployments took hours, environments drifted, and two outages that year came from configuration differences. Task: As tech lead for the platform, I was asked to propose a better hosting model. Action: I defined criteria: deployment speed, cost, team skills, operational overhead and portability. I evaluated three options: improving the VM automation, Amazon ECS, and Amazon EKS. We ran a two-week proof of concept moving one service to both ECS and EKS, measuring deploy times and the effort of autoscaling and secrets. I wrote an RFC with a cost model, recommending EKS because we needed portability for an upcoming on-premise customer and the team already knew Kubernetes basics; I flagged its higher operational complexity. Reviews with architects, security and four product teams added requirements like network policies and a managed node approach, which I incorporated. The decision went into an ADR and we migrated service by service over six months. Result: Deployment time dropped from hours to under ten minutes, configuration-drift incidents stopped, and infrastructure cost fell about 25% within a year through better bin-packing and autoscaling.

Common mistakes

  • Presenting the decision as obvious, with no alternatives.
  • "I decided and told everyone."
  • Opinion without data or prototype.
  • No measurable result.
  • Hiding the downsides of the chosen option.

How to answer in the interview

"I drove the move from self-managed VMs to Kubernetes on EKS. I set criteria like cost, team skills, portability and operational load, and compared three options: improving the VMs, ECS and EKS. We ran a two-week proof of concept with benchmarks on one service. I wrote an RFC, reviewed it with architects, security and the teams that would use it, and incorporated their concerns, which gave real buy-in rather than a top-down decision, and recorded it as an ADR. Deployments went from hours to minutes, and costs dropped about 25% within a year."

Likely follow-up questions

  • A decision that turned out wrong? Describe the signal, how you reversed or adjusted, and the learning.
  • Someone senior strongly disagreed? Understand their concern, test it with data, escalate with both views if needed, then disagree and commit.
  • When don't you write an RFC? For reversible, local decisions; RFCs are for costly-to-reverse choices.
