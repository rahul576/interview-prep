What risks come with using LLMs in incident response, and how do you mitigate them?

AI Agents & AIOps · concept · levels senior, lead

In one line

The main risks are confident but wrong explanations, automation acting on wrong conclusions, sensitive data sent to models and over-reliance eroding team knowledge; mitigate by requiring evidence for every claim, presenting ranked hypotheses, keeping humans in charge of decisions, redacting data and reviewing AI contributions in postmortems.

Detailed explanation

Incidents are high-pressure situations where people look for quick answers. An AI that states "the root cause is the Redis upgrade" with confidence can send responders down the wrong path for half an hour, while the real cause goes unexamined. Worse, if automation acts on such conclusions, it might roll back an innocent service or restart healthy components. LLMs can misread graphs, confuse correlated timing with causation and invent connections between unrelated log lines.

Data exposure is the second risk. Logs and traces contain personal data, tokens and customer information. Sending them to an external model provider may violate policy or regulation, so data must be redacted and only approved models used. A third risk is subtle: over-reliance. If the AI always investigates, engineers stop building their mental model of the system and are lost when the AI fails. Prompt injection through log content or ticket text is a further risk for agents with tools.

Mitigations: every claim must link to the query, graph or log line that supports it; findings are ranked hypotheses with confidence, not verdicts; humans decide, and automation is limited to approved low-risk actions; sensitive data is redacted; and postmortems record where AI helped or misled, improving prompts and training responders to use it critically.

How it works

  1. AI investigation runs with read-only tools only.
  2. Output format enforces evidence links per claim and a confidence level.
  3. Incident commander decides actions; AI suggestions are labelled as such.
  4. Redaction layer filters PII and secrets before model calls.
  5. Postmortem template includes "AI assistance: helpful / misleading / neutral".

Example

AI findings (hypotheses, verify before acting)
H1 [medium] payments-db CPU saturated since 14:05  -> query: [link]  graph: [link]
H2 [low]    Redis upgrade 13:40 — no error increase on Redis clients -> [link]
Not supported by evidence: "network issue" (no packet loss on mesh metrics) [link]

Common mistakes

  • Letting AI output appear as a verdict in the incident channel.
  • Allowing remediation based on AI conclusions without approval.
  • Sending raw logs to unapproved external models.
  • Never reviewing whether AI help was accurate.

How to answer in the interview

"The biggest risk is a confident but wrong explanation that misdirects responders, and worse, automation acting on it. Logs sent to models can leak personal data or secrets, and over time teams can rely on AI so much they lose their own understanding. I mitigate by requiring every claim to link to the actual evidence, presenting ranked hypotheses rather than verdicts, keeping decisions with humans and limiting automation to approved low-risk actions, redacting sensitive data with approved models, and reviewing AI contributions in every postmortem."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Training responders? Game days where the AI is deliberately wrong.
  • Prompt injection via logs? Treat tool output as data and keep agents read-only.
  • Measure misdirection? Track time lost following wrong hypotheses.
