What are the main EBS volume types, and how do you choose?

Cloud (AWS) · concept · levels entry

In one line

gp3 (general-purpose SSD) is the default for most workloads and lets you set IOPS and throughput independently of size; io2 / io2 Block Express (and older io1) are provisioned-IOPS SSDs for demanding databases; st1 is throughput-optimised HDD for large sequential reads, and sc1 is cold HDD for rarely accessed data.

Detailed explanation

EBS volume types trade off IOPS (small random operations, important for databases), throughput (MB/s for large sequential reads and writes), latency, durability and cost.

gp3 is the right starting point for boot volumes, application servers, development databases and many production databases. It includes a baseline of 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s at any size, and you can provision more IOPS and throughput separately from capacity, up to much higher limits (AWS raised gp3 maximums in 2025, so check the current documentation for exact figures). Its predecessor gp2 tied performance to size (3 IOPS per GB with burst credits), so people over-provisioned disk just to get IOPS; moving gp2 to gp3 is usually cheaper and often faster.

io2 (and the older io1) are provisioned IOPS SSDs for I/O-intensive, latency-sensitive workloads such as large OLTP databases (Oracle, SQL Server, busy PostgreSQL). You pay for the IOPS you provision, get consistent sub-millisecond latency and, with io2, higher durability (99.999%). io2 Block Express offers the highest performance, large volumes and Multi-Attach support.

st1 (throughput-optimised HDD) suits big sequential workloads like log processing, Kafka, data warehouses and ETL. sc1 (cold HDD) is the cheapest per GB for infrequently accessed data. HDD types cannot be boot volumes.

Choose from the workload pattern, then monitor CloudWatch metrics: VolumeReadOps/WriteOps, throughput, VolumeQueueLength, average latency and, on Nitro instances, whether the instance's EBS bandwidth is the real bottleneck. Elastic Volumes let you change type, size, IOPS and throughput online without detaching.

How it works

  1. Measure required IOPS, throughput and latency.
  2. Start with gp3; tune IOPS and throughput.
  3. Use io2 when you need high, consistent IOPS and low latency.
  4. Use st1 or sc1 for sequential or cold data.
  5. Check instance-level EBS limits.
  6. Modify online with Elastic Volumes as needs change.

Example

# Convert a gp2 volume to gp3 with extra IOPS, online
aws ec2 modify-volume --volume-id vol-0abc123 \
  --volume-type gp3 --iops 6000 --throughput 250

aws ec2 describe-volumes-modifications --volume-ids vol-0abc123

A 1 TB gp2 volume gave 3,000 IOPS; as gp3 the same size costs less per GB, and the team provisions exactly the 6,000 IOPS the database needs.

Common mistakes

  • Staying on gp2 and buying extra GB just to get IOPS.
  • Choosing io2 by default and overpaying.
  • Ignoring the instance's maximum EBS bandwidth.
  • Trying to boot from st1 or sc1.

How to answer in the interview

"gp3 is my default for boot volumes and most applications; unlike gp2 I can set IOPS and throughput independently of size, which is usually cheaper. For demanding databases that need high, consistent IOPS and low latency, I'd use io2 or io2 Block Express. For large sequential workloads like log processing I'd use st1, and sc1 for cold, rarely accessed data at the lowest cost. I choose from the workload pattern, watch latency, queue length and throughput in CloudWatch, check the instance's EBS bandwidth limit, and can change type or size online with Elastic Volumes."

Likely follow-up questions

  • Why migrate gp2 to gp3? Lower price per GB and performance decoupled from size, so you stop over-provisioning.
  • What limits EBS performance besides the volume? The instance type's EBS bandwidth and IOPS limits.
  • Is changing volume type disruptive? No, Elastic Volumes modify it online; optimisation runs in the background.
